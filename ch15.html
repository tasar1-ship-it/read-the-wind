The last chapter divided the beat into thirds. This one is about the other boats, and how they change your choices.

15.1Clear air is worth a lot

Clear air is worth more than a small gain of angle. Dirty air costs about four lengths a minute, and a few degrees of angle are not worth that. In a fleet of seventy the dirty air is everywhere, and the boat that spends the beat in clean wind gains four lengths on every boat that spent a minute of it in someone's shadow. More pressure you can see, or a real shift, can be worth more: then you weigh them (section 6.5).

So a move is judged first by the air it leaves you in. A tack that puts you in a lane of clean wind is good even if the angle is a little worse. A tack that puts you just behind and below another boat is bad even if it points you at the mark. Before every tack, look at where you will be and whose wind you will be in.

Keep the lane you have while it is clean (chapter 13). When it dies, leave early. And when you choose between two places to be, the one with the fewest boats upwind of it is usually right.

There is one more side to clear air: giving it. Every boat you tack in front of is a boat you have slowed, and in a big fleet that is sometimes the point (section 15.6, and section 16.4 says how much room a tack in front needs). But most of the time your own clean wind is worth more than anyone else's dirty air.

On the waterIn your next beat, before every tack, say out loud whose wind you will be in after it: “clean” or the sail number of the boat whose shadow you would land in. Tack only on “clean” unless you have a reason you can say. You’ve got it when you can say after the race which boat's shadow you sat in and for about how long, and your coach agrees.

15.2Stay with the leaders, but think

When the leading group is upwind of you, it is in the wind that is coming to you. Watch them: the gusts they heel to and the shifts that lift them are yours a minute later. A fleet that leans one way is telling you something about the wind over there, and the best sailors in it are usually there for a reason.

So stay in touch with them: close enough in leverage that a shift cannot take you out of the race, on the side of the course they are using unless you have a reason of your own that you can see. A boat that goes to the opposite corner from the leaders with no evidence is not racing them, it is hoping.

But do not copy blindly. The leaders are in the wind that is on them now, not the wind coming from beyond them, and a leader on the wrong side of a new shift is still a leader for another minute. When the fleet splits, look for the reason: new pressure on one side, a shift the front boats have felt. Follow the evidence, not the biggest group.

The sentence to say has three parts, like the sentences you say in chapter 14: “leaders going right, more wind there, I go right.” The middle part is the one that matters. If you cannot fill it in, you are copying.

On the waterIn your next beat, say out loud every minute what the leading group is doing and why: “leaders right, dark water there.” If you cannot say why, say so. You’ve got it when after the race you can say which side the leaders used and what the reason was, and your coach agrees with the reason.

15.3Bank a gain

A shift goes your way and you gain seven lengths on the boats out to the other side. The gain is real, but it is on loan. It exists only while the leverage that made it exists, and the next shift can take it back.

Banking it means taking away the leverage: cross the boats you have gained on, and come back towards them. Now you are between them and the mark, close enough that the next shift moves you all the same way, and the seven lengths are yours whatever the wind does.

The moment to bank is as soon as the gain is clear and you can cross them. If you are still sailing away from them, the shift that gained you is a header on the tack you are on, so the tack towards them is the lifted tack anyway: chapter 3's rule and banking agree. Tack, cross their bows, and tack back in front of them, even though that second tack is the headed one for a while. Do not sail on waiting for the next shift: the next shift is the one going back, and it starts taking the gain away the moment it arrives. The sailor who sails on to the layline to make the gain bigger usually watches it go.

Banking is not the same as giving up the side. If the side is still paying, more pressure you can see or a one-way day, you can cross the fleet and still lead them back towards it, a little at a time. You keep the position and the plan; you give up only the leverage you no longer need.

On the waterIn your next race, when a shift gains you a clear few lengths on boats to one side, say “bank it” out loud, tack towards them and cross while the shift is still in. You’ve got it when after the race you can name one gain you banked and where you crossed, and the gain was still there at the mark.

15.4Don't be the outside boat

Look across the course and find the boat furthest out to one side. That is the , and she carries the biggest risk in the fleet. She has the most leverage on everyone, so the next shift gains or loses her the most, and from a she has one way to the mark and no reply to a header.

Sometimes she is right, and she wins the beat by twenty lengths. Far more often she is not, because the shift she needs has to come while she is out there, and nobody controls that. A sailor who is often the outside boat has some great races and many bad ones, and a series is scored on all of them.

So stay inside the fleet's spread unless you are sure: inside the outside boats on both sides, with leverage on the rivals that matter but not on the whole fleet. If you find yourself becoming the outside boat without having chosen it, that is a sign that the fleet knows something you do not. Come in.

When you are sure, when there is more wind you can see on one side or a one-way day, go out, and go early, before the fleet blocks the way. Being the outside boat on purpose, with evidence, is a decision. Being it by accident is a mistake.

On the waterIn your next beat, every two minutes, find the outside boat on each side and say whether you are inside both of them. You’ve got it when after the race you can say who the outside boats were and that you were inside them unless you chose not to be, and can say why you chose.

15.5How much risk? It depends on the race

Leverage is a bet, and the right size of bet depends on two things: how sure you are of the wind, and where you are in the race. A gain of seven lengths changes nothing for a leader and everything for a boat far behind; a loss of seven does the opposite. The race decides how much you can afford to lose; how sure you are decides how much is worth risking.

Leading, or protecting a good series: low risk. Stay between your rivals and the mark, keep clear air, stay away from the laylines, and let the race come to you. In the middle of the fleet with the wind uncertain: medium. Centre of the course, play the shifts, no blind corners.

Behind with little race left: higher. Look for a different lane of pressure or a shift the fleet has not used; copying the leaders cannot pass them. A side you are sure of, more wind you can see: go, and go early, before the fleet blocks the way. A dark patch far away that may be nothing: move towards it only while the route stays flexible.

The card's last line (chapter 9) is the same question asked before the start: careful, normal or bold. Say it again at the first mark, because the race has changed by then, and the right size of bet has changed with it.

On the waterBefore your next race, say the risk line on your card, and at the first mark say it again: “careful”, “normal” or “bold”, and why. You’ve got it when after the race you can say the two risk words and whether what you did on the beats matched them.

15.6Loose cover or your own race

When a rival is behind you, you can her: sail so that she cannot pass without sailing through your wind or past you. There are two ways, and only one of them is usually right.

A loose cover keeps you between her and the mark, or between her and the next advantage, with your own clear air and your own shifts. She cannot pass without a shift or a gust of her own, and because you are upwind of her with little leverage between you, most of what reaches her reaches you first. You lose almost nothing, and you keep the whole fleet in view.

A tight cover sits on her wind: you tack when she tacks and stay in front of her and above her. It slows you both, every tack costs a length or two, and while the two of you tack together the rest of the fleet sails the shifts and passes you both. Use it only when controlling that one boat is worth more than your place in the fleet: the last race of a series, one rival, one point.

The rest of the time, sail your own race, with a loose cover on the boats that matter. Chapter 16 says what to do when the rival is not behind you but coming at you.

On the waterIn your next race, when a rival is behind you, say which cover you are using, “loose” or “tight”, and why. Use “tight” only when you can name the race reason. You’ve got it when after the race you can say which boats you covered and how, and you did not tack on a rival's wind without a reason you can name.

15.7Protect or attack

Every beat has a moment when the question changes from “how do I gain” to “how do I keep what I have”. Most sailors miss it, because attacking is more fun. The moment comes when the gain you could still make is smaller than the loss you could still take.

Protect when you are ahead and the race is more than half over; when you are ahead of the rivals that matter for the series; when you have banked a gain and the fleet is coming back towards you; when the wind has gone steady and there are no more shifts to win. Protecting means a loose cover, clear air, the middle, no laylines, and the last two tacks planned early.

Attack when you are behind with little to lose; when there is a real opportunity you can see, pressure or a shift the fleet has not used; when the race is young and a bad beat can still be mended. Attacking means leverage, chosen on purpose, towards something you can see.

Say which one you are doing. “Protecting: between them and the mark, clear air.” “Attacking: right, for the pressure.” The sailor who is attacking by habit while leading, or protecting by habit while last, has stopped thinking.

On the waterAt the first mark of your next race, say “protect” or “attack” out loud, with the reason, and sail the next beat that way. You’ve got it when after the race you can say which word you chose at the first mark and why, and the next beat you sailed matched it.

15.8What can hurt me next?

Once a minute, ask one question: what can hurt me next? It is the defensive scan, and it takes ten seconds. Three things can hurt you on a beat, and the scan looks for each.

A boat: one that can cross you and tack on your wind, or one coming at you on starboard when you are on port, or one climbing up from below into your lane. Look under the boom as well: your sail can hide her. For each: where will she be in a minute, and what do I do if she does it?

A shift: the wind going the wrong way for the side you are on. For it: how big is my leverage, and would a shift against me still leave me in the race? If not, reduce the leverage now, while it is cheap.

A layline or a corner you are approaching: the end of your choices. For it: how far away is it, and have I decided the last two tacks (chapter 14)? Say the scan out loud: “boat above tacking, shift against me small, layline far: fine.” The race goes wrong one unexpected thing at a time, and the scan is what makes them expected.

On the waterIn your next beat, once a minute, say the scan out loud: the boat that could hurt you, the shift that could, the layline or corner ahead, and what you would do about each. You’ve got it when your coach hears the scan once a minute in training, and after the race you can name one thing the scan saw coming before it happened.
