Upwind racing starts with one question: where is the wind? It is never the same all over the course. Some patches have more, some have less, and the boats in the good patches pull away from the rest. This chapter teaches you to see the wind on the water, to look for it every 20 seconds, and to tell a real gust from a clue that fools you. Chapter 2 is about the other half: which way the wind is blowing.

1.1The wind is patchy

Look up the course on any day with wind. The water is not all the same colour. Some patches are darker and rougher: there is more wind there. Those are . Other patches are paler and smoother: there is less wind there. Those are .

More wind makes the water look darker, because it covers the water with more and bigger ripples. The darkest, roughest water has the most wind. Smooth, shiny water has almost none.

The patches do not stay still. They blow down the course with the wind. A gust you see straight up the course from you reaches you a little later, and the boats in front of you get it first. A gust off to one side passes you by, unless you sail towards it.

Sailors call the strength of the wind . “More pressure on the left” means more wind on the left as you look up the course towards the mark. In an Optimist, more pressure usually means more speed (section 1.3), so finding it is the first job on every .

On the waterBefore you launch, or while you wait for your start, watch the water where you will race for two minutes. Point at a dark patch and name the boat it will reach first. You’ve got it when the boat you named or speeds up as the patch arrives, three times in a row.

1.2Count to 20 and look up the course

Upwind, your eyes want to stay on the sail and the . That keeps you fast, but it also keeps you blind to everything that is coming. So build a habit: count to 20, look far up the course, to the mark and beyond, for two or three seconds, then go back to the sail.

Look at the same things in the same order every time. The water: where is it darker, and will the dark patches reach you or pass you by? The boats across the course: are the ones on the left, in the middle or on the right more or going faster? and your bow: has anything changed (chapter 2)? The sky and the shore: is a big cloud coming that could bring more wind, or take it away? And every few looks, the one place your sail hides: drop your head and look under the boom, on your sail's side. A boat coming from that side can stay behind your sail all the way in.

Twenty seconds is short enough that hardly any gust reaches you unseen, and long enough that your speed does not suffer. Keep steering, and keep the boat at the same heel while you look.

Then say what you saw in three words: “more wind left”, “more wind right” or “same both sides”. Saying it makes you decide, and deciding is what the look is for.

On the waterOn one whole beat in training, sailing level with a partner, count to 20, look up the course and say your three words out loud, every time. You’ve got it when you make the call on every count of 20 up to the mark, and your partner has not pulled away.

1.3More wind usually wins

An Optimist is small and light, so a little more wind makes a big difference. In a gust you go faster and you can point higher, both at once. That is why the boats in the dark patches pull away from the rest.

So when the look shows more wind on one side, go and get it. It is usually worth sailing a few extra boat lengths to reach a gust, and then staying in it for as long as you can. When it fades, look for the next one: good sailors hop from patch to patch all the way up the beat. One kind of cloud is different. A big, dark, tall cloud can bring a sudden strong gust, rain or thunder: never sail towards it for its wind. Watch your coach and the committee boat for a signal to come in (ask your coach which one your club uses), and if you see lightning or hear thunder, stop racing, stay close to your coach and do what the coach boat tells you.

It matters most in light wind, when a little more wind is a big part of all the wind there is.

Usually, not always. More wind stops helping when you already have so much that you cannot keep the boat flat. And a gust comes down the course much faster than you can sail across to it, so you can only catch one that is well up the course and not far to the side.

A patch very far away may be gone before you get there. If you are not sure a patch is real, move towards it only as far as you can still change your mind, and never into a corner. And more wind on one side has to be weighed against a better angle on the other: chapter 4 shows how.

On the waterOn each beat, pick a dark patch you can reach for a few extra boat lengths, and sail for it. You’ve got it when you reach the patch while it still has its ripples, and the boats that were level with you in lighter wind drop behind.

1.4Clues that can fool you

Not everything dark is wind. Before you sail for a patch, check what made it dark.

A cloud between the sun and the water casts a shadow, and the shadow looks dark too. Deep water, weed and a dark hill reflected in the water can look dark as well. Ripples tell the truth where colour does not. A gust has more ripples than the water around it. A shadow has the same ripples, only darker. A second test: a gust moves down the course with the wind, while deep water, weed and a reflected hill stay put. And looking towards the sun, a gust can sparkle instead of looking darker.

Boats are an even better test than water. If the boats in a patch more and speed up, the wind is real. Watch two or three of them, not one: a light sailor heels sooner than a heavy one, and a good sailor is fast anyway.

Clues on land need care too. A flag on the shore shows the wind on the shore, where buildings and trees bend and slow it. A boat at anchor is pulled by the current as well as the wind, so it may not point into the wind. Use them for a first guess, then trust the water and the boats.

And smooth, shiny water is a hole with almost no wind in it. Sail round it.

On the waterEach time you call a dark patch, check before you go: does it have more ripples than the water around it, and are any boats in it heeling, hiking harder or speeding up? You’ve got it when you say “real” or “only darker” for every patch before you go, and the patches you called real still have the wind when you get there.

1.5Gust lanes and the shore

Near the shore, the land shapes the wind. When the wind blows off the land, it does not arrive evenly. Much of it pours through the valleys between the hills and fans out over the water, so the gusts come down the course in lanes of darker water, with lighter wind between them.

A lane like that is a . It stays where the land puts it for as long as the wind keeps its direction. So if the same side of the course keeps getting the dark water, it is not luck. Go there, and keep checking that the lane is still working.

Close under high land, buildings or trees the wind is light and patchy: that is a , and it reaches a long way out. Stay out of it unless something else on that side is worth more.

Two more patterns are worth knowing. Round the end of a point of land the wind is often stronger, squeezed round the corner. And wind blowing in from the sea, like the , is usually steadier than wind off the land. Chapter 4 shows what the embat does at your club.

On the waterOn a day when the wind blows off the land, look at the land upwind of the course before the start. Find the valleys, the high ground and the points, then watch where the dark lanes come down. You’ve got it when you can point at a lane on the water and at the gap in the land it comes from, and before the start you say where the lanes are.
