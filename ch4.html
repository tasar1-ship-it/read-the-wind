Chapter 2 taught you to see a shift. Chapter 3 taught you why the lifted tack wins. But there is one kind of day on which “tack on the header” is wrong. This chapter shows you the three kinds of day, how to tell them apart before the start, what the embat does in your bay, and when more wind beats a better angle.

4.1Swinging shifts: tack on the headers

On most days the wind swings back and forth around a middle direction: a few degrees to the left, then a few to the right, then left again. Each swing lasts a few minutes, and then the wind swings back. Sailors call this a . Both sides of the course take turns gaining: first the right, then the left, then the right again.

That is the day for the rule from chapter 3: sail the , and on the real . Three things make the rule work.

Know your (section 2.5), and tack when your bow is clearly lower than normal and the header has stayed, not on a wobble (section 2.4).

Stay near the middle of the course. From the middle you can use whichever swing comes next. Out at a the next swing is wasted, or it hurts, and there is nothing you can do about it.

Do not chase every swing. A tack costs a boat length or two, so a swing that is gone in half a minute is not worth one: the tack would not have paid for itself before the wind swung back (section 2.4). Tack on the clear headers, and in between keep the boat fast.

The biggest losers on a swinging day are the boats that go to a . They bank one lift, and then they watch the wind swing back against them with no way to answer.

On the waterOn a swinging day, tack on every real header, and each time you tack look at the mark. If it sits nearer to square across your boat than to your bow, you are past the middle and sailing out towards a layline: take the next header early. You’ve got it when you reach a layline only on your last tack into the mark, and every other tack was on a header that stayed, or an early one back towards the middle.

4.2One-way shifts: go towards the shift

Some days the wind does not swing back. It keeps turning the same way, hour after hour: a . The embat does this slowly through the afternoon (section 4.5), and a change in the weather can do it fast.

On a one-way day the ladder keeps turning the same way, so the boats on the side the wind is turning towards keep gaining. So go to that side first, even on a header.

Say the wind is turning right. Get onto soon after the gun, going right. Port is being headed, and it feels wrong: your bow keeps swinging away from the mark. Keep going. Tack a little before the layline, while the mark is still just ahead of square: the turn still to come will lift you up to it. Now you are on starboard, and the shift that keeps coming lifts you all the way to the mark.

The boat that tacked on the first header went left, and every bit of the shift hurt it. When it finally tacks to come back, it is on the headed tack, with the whole shift against it.

Two cautions. Be sure before you commit: one header is not a one-way day, and section 4.4 shows how to tell. And go towards the shift step by step, never to the layline: tack there and the next lift takes you past the mark, and if the wind stops turning you want choices left. Before the start, say out loud which way the wind is turning.

On the waterAfter the gun, sail first towards the side the wind is turning to, on the headed tack if you have to, and tack a little before the layline, while the mark is still just ahead of square. You’ve got it when after your tack you are lifted all the way to the mark without easing your sheet, and the boats that tacked on the first header round behind you.

4.3No clear pattern

Some days you cannot tell. The swings have no rhythm, each side gains once and then loses once, or you simply have not seen enough yet. A big guess on a day like this is a bet with your whole race.

So bet small. Stay near the middle of the course and near the boats you are racing: a few boat lengths of , not a whole side. Keep clear wind, because in you can neither see a shift nor use it (section 2.4).

Sail the first: the tack that points closer to the mark. It keeps you nearer the middle for longer, and you have to sail more of it anyway. If the lifted tack is the short one, sail it only while it is clearly lifted, and never to its layline.

Tack on the clear headers, as on a swinging day, but keep both laylines open. A boat in the middle with the mark still far away has every choice left.

And keep looking. The pattern often shows itself on the first beat: the swings keep coming back, or one of them never does. The moment you can name the day, race it the way sections 4.1 and 4.2 say.

On the waterWhen you cannot say what the wind is doing, say “not sure” out loud, sail the long tack first, and stay within a few boat lengths of the boats around you. You’ve got it when halfway up the beat you still have both laylines open, clear wind, and the boats you are racing within a few boat lengths.

4.4Find out before the start

The time before the start is for finding out what kind of day it is.

Sail on one tack for about a minute, tack, and do the same on the other, three times over. Each time, watch your bow against your far-away point (section 2.5), and say where it is: left of the point, on it, or right of it. Keep looking until the start: every look is one more point on the line.

Then read the pattern. Left of the point, then right, then left again, around the same middle: a swinging day. Moving the same way each time you look: a one-way day, so say which way. No pattern you can see: say “not sure” and race the way section 4.3 says. Notice how big the swings are: a header must be bigger than the usual wobble to be worth a tack.

Two more clues help. On a one-way day the fleet slowly leans towards one side as the sailors feel it. And near the line, point your bow straight into the wind: if one end comes further upwind each time you check, the wind is turning towards that end. If it is the same amount every time, that is only the line, set at an angle.

Write your call down before the start: swinging, one-way left, one-way right, or not sure. After the race, compare it with what the wind really did. That is where you learn.

No pattern lasts for ever. A swinging morning can turn into a one-way afternoon when the embat arrives. Check again before every start.

On the waterBefore every start, sail each tack for a minute, three times over, watching your bow against your point. Then say the kind of day out loud: “swinging”, “one-way left”, “one-way right” or “not sure”. You’ve got it when your call before the start matches what the wind did on the first beat, in three races out of four.

4.5The embat at your club

The is the sea breeze of Mallorca. On a sunny day from spring to early autumn, the land heats up faster than the sea. The warm air over the land rises, and cool air from the sea flows in to take its place. That flow is the embat.

In your bay it comes in from the sea, roughly from the south-west, around midday, as a dark line on the water spreading towards the shore. It starts light, builds through the afternoon to 10 to 15 knots, is strongest at about three or four o’clock, and dies away towards evening.

It is a steady wind, with smaller swings than wind off the land.

But the embat has one habit that matters. As the afternoon goes on, it usually turns to the right, a little every hour: only a few degrees in one beat, a very slow one-way shift (section 4.2). Still tack on the clear headers, but when nothing else decides it, lean right. Check it: your bay has its own shape, and hills and headlands bend the breeze.

Three warnings. If the morning wind off the land is strong, the embat may not come at all. If it is light, a hole of no wind can sit between the two. And a cloud over the land can switch the embat off in minutes.

If one side keeps winning race after race, the land is shaping the wind. Use that side, and check each race that it still works.

On the waterOn every embat day, note three things when you come ashore: the time it arrived, where it came from, and which way it turned during the afternoon. You’ve got it when after five embat days you can say before the start where the embat will come from, when, and which way it will turn, and you are right.

4.6More wind or a better angle?

Two things can pull you to different sides: more wind on one side, a better angle on the other. In an Optimist, more wind usually wins, and the bigger the shift, the more wind it takes.

The rule: go for the wind when you can see it and it stays. Darker water that is still there at your next count of 20, with boats in it heeling and going faster, is worth a small loss of angle, and in light wind a big one. Go for the angle when the wind looks the same on both sides. And when wind and angle point the same way, go: that is the strongest signal you will get all day.

Now the two cases chapter 2 left open. A header that comes with a : look before you tack. The gust may have brought its own direction, and that direction leaves with it. Tack only if the other tack keeps you in the dark water. If tacking would take you out of the gust, hold on, take the header, and stay in the wind.

One side of the course that keeps gaining: that is more wind on that side, or the land bending the wind (sections 1.5 and 4.5). It is not a shift to tack on. Choose the side, sail to it, and stay there while it keeps working.

In light wind, wind is everything. In a good breeze, when every boat is already going fast, the angle counts for more.

On the waterEach time wind and angle disagree, say which you chose and why: “wind, it stays”, “angle, it will not stay” or “angle, we are all fast already”. You’ve got it when the boats that chose the other way come in behind you more often than not, and each time you can say why.
