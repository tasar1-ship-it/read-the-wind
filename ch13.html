The gun has gone. Part III got you to the line in the front row with a lane; this chapter is the first minute after it, when the start is kept or given back. It is about speed before anything else, checking whether the start gave you your lane, seeing a lane die and leaving it early, the first escape when you want the right, keeping the starboard lane when you want the left, when ducking a few boats buys a better lane, why the panic tack loses, and how the first minute connects back to the plan on your card.

13.1Speed first

For the first thirty seconds after the gun there is one job: speed. Flat boat, sheet in, hike, eyes on the front of the sail, the water ahead and the boats close to you. Not the far-away fleet, not the mark, not the boat that got the end. A slow boat cannot point and cannot steer, and every boat around you is fast.

Speed first means sailing a little lower than your best angle for those thirty seconds, with the sail full, until the boat is at full speed and the wake is straight. Then come up to your normal angle. A boat that tries to point from the gun : the front of the sail goes soft, the boat slows, and slides sideways into the boat below.

Speed first also means no decisions. The three checks at the gun (chapter 11) need no thought: legal and fast in the first fifteen seconds, the lane at thirty. Everything else waits. A boat that is thinking about tacking in the first ten seconds is not thinking about the sail.

At thirty seconds, look up at the fleet. By then the row has sorted itself: the fast boats are a length ahead, the slow ones are already in dirty air, and you can see which you are. That is the moment the rest of this chapter begins.

On the waterIn your next three starts, say “legal” at the gun: if you are not sure, one glance at the committee boat and a listen. Then until thirty seconds keep your eyes moving between the front of the sail, the water ahead and the boats close to you, and say nothing except “flat, fast”. Leave the far-away fleet until thirty. You’ve got it when your coach sees the boat flat and you looking at the sail, the water and the boats close by for the first thirty seconds, and at thirty you are level with or ahead of the boats beside you.

13.2Did the start give you your lane?

At thirty seconds, one question: did the start give me my ? Look at the boat below you and the boat above you, and at your own speed against theirs.

Your lane is good when three things are true. Nobody is ahead and to windward of you, so your wind is clean. The boat below you is level with you or behind, not creeping ahead. And you are holding your angle without : the sail is full and the boat feels free.

If it is good, use it. Settle into your normal angle, keep the boat flat, and sail. Look ahead every few seconds for the next thing: a boat above that is dropping down, a gust, the first shift. A good lane is worth a lot in a big fleet, and the right move is usually to keep it, even when the plan said tack early, until the fleet spreads out and the tack is free.

If it is not good, the next section is for you. But be honest about which it is. A lane that is a little slow but clean is a good lane; a lane where you point lower every few seconds is a dying one, and the difference decides the first beat.

On the waterAt thirty seconds after your next gun, say out loud “good lane” or “dying”, with the reason: “clean above, level below” or “she is climbing”. You’ve got it when you say the call at thirty seconds in every start, and a minute later it has turned out right in most of them.

13.3When your lane is dying

A lane dies from below or from above. From below: the boat to leeward gets her bow ahead and starts climbing towards you, her bent wind on your sail, the . From above: the boat to windward drops down towards your track and her dirty air falls on you. Either way you point lower, go slower, and slide towards the boat below.

The signs are in your hands and your eyes. You are slower than the boat beside you for several lengths. You have to pinch to stay off the boat below. You are using the tiller all the time just to stay where you are. Any one of these, for more than a few seconds, means the lane is dying.

Leave early. The loss grows every second you wait, and a lane does not recover by hoping. Three ways out: tack, if there is room and the new tack leads to usable wind (section 13.4); off a little, when the trouble is the boat above you and there is open water below you, so that you build speed out from under her; or, when the trouble is a boat climbing from below, hold for a few seconds in a slightly higher mode to keep the gap, only while the lane is still usable, and never as a long slow pinch.

Count it. Your coach can time the seconds from the moment a lane is clearly dying to the moment you change something. Ten seconds is good. Thirty is a habit to break.

On the waterIn your next race, when your lane starts dying, say “dying” out loud and change something within ten seconds: tack, foot off from a boat above if there is water below, or point up for a moment against a boat below. You’ve got it when your coach times you at ten seconds or less from the lane dying to the change, in most of your starts.

13.4Going right: your first escape

If the card says right, the first minute is about finding the first safe tack onto port. Safe means two things: a hole behind you with no starboard boat in it, because on port you keep clear of every boat on starboard S1, and usable wind on the other tack, not another boat's dirty air.

From the committee boat end the hole is there at the gun (chapter 9). From anywhere else it opens as the fleet spreads: the boat above you sails on ahead and the boats behind her drop back, so after a minute or two there is a space of two or three lengths behind her to tack into. Watch for it every few seconds from thirty seconds on: look back over your shoulder, to windward and behind you.

When it comes, go: a clean tack, flat and fast, and then speed first again on port, because the boats you pass behind leave dirty air too. Do not wait for a perfect hole with nobody near; take the first one that needs no , or one duck at most.

If the hole does not come and your lane is good, keep the lane and keep looking: the right is not going anywhere, and a boat in clear air on starboard is still gaining on the boats in dirty air around her. If the hole does not come and your lane is dying, section 13.6 is the way across.

On the waterWhen your card says right, from thirty seconds after the gun look back over your shoulder, to windward and behind you, every few seconds and say “hole” when you see two lengths of water behind the boat above you. Tack into the first one that needs no duck. You’ve got it when when your card says right, you are on port within two minutes of the gun in most starts, and you can say how many boats you ducked (none, or one).

13.5Going left: keep your lane

If the card says left, the first minute is simpler: hold starboard, keep the lane, and let the boats that tack early go. Every boat that tacks out of the row leaves more room and cleaner wind for the boats that stay.

Keeping the lane means watching two boats. The boat below: stay level with her or ahead, and if she starts to climb, point a little higher for a few seconds to keep the gap between you (section 16.5), or tack if that takes you where you want. Footing off only takes you down onto her. The boat above: if she drops down towards you, hold your angle and watch her; while you are overlapped, she is the one who has to keep clear S2, and if she does not, you turn away in good time. If her dirty air reaches you and there is water below, foot off (section 13.3). Most of the time she will tack away to find her own lane.

But a starboard lane has an end. Hold it long enough and you reach the left (chapter 17), where you have to tack onto port to fetch the mark, and every boat coming in to the mark on starboard from the right has right of way over you S1. And a boat close above you can take away your room to tack. So keep an eye on two things: how far the layline is ahead, and whether you still have space to windward.

The left is a plan, not a corner. Sail towards the wind or the shift you chose, and tack before the layline, while the mark is still ahead of square across your boat (chapter 4; chapter 3 gives the layline itself). The lane got you there; the plan says when to leave it.

On the waterWhen your card says left, hold starboard after the gun and say out loud each time a boat near you tacks away: “one gone, more room.” Tack only on your own reason: a header, more wind, or the layline coming. You’ve got it when when your card says left, you are still on starboard in clear air two minutes after the gun, or you can say the reason you tacked earlier.

13.6Duck a few boats for a better lane

Sometimes the lane is dying, the right is your side, and there is no hole. Then the question is whether to tack now and the boats behind you to get across. A duck costs about a length each. A dying lane costs a length every fifteen seconds and keeps costing.

So count the boats. One or two ducks for a lane of clear air on the side you want is a good trade: two lengths now against four a minute later and the side you chose. Five ducks is not: you arrive on the right five lengths down, in the dirty air of all five boats you ducked, and still losing.

The duck itself has a technique. Bear away early, with speed, so you pass close behind her stern and lose as little height as possible; sheet in and come back up as her stern passes. A late duck is a slow one, because you have to turn hard, and a slow boat loses more than a length.

Decide at thirty seconds and again at one minute, with the count: how many ducks to the clear lane on my side? Two or fewer and it is worth it: go. More, and stay on this tack: use the other ways out in section 13.3 while you look for the hole (section 13.4).

On the waterWhen your lane is dying and you want the right, count the boats you would have to duck and say the number out loud. Two or fewer: tack and go. Three or more: foot off if there is water below, and look for a hole. You’ve got it when after each such start you can say the number you counted and whether the lane on the other side was clean when you got there.

13.7No panic tacks

A poor start hurts, and the first thing the body wants to do is tack: get away, do something. The panic tack in the first ten seconds is the worst move on the course. You go onto port into a wall of starboard boats, you keep clear of all of them S1, and you arrive on the other side further behind than you started, with your plan gone.

A poor start is a few lengths. It is not the race. The boats around you are all in each other's dirty air too, and the ones in the second row who sail fast for a minute pass many of the ones in the front row who did not.

So after a poor start do what you would do after a good one: speed first for thirty seconds, then the question. Is there open water anywhere near? Foot off into it, accept the small loss and rebuild. Is there a hole behind for a clean tack? Take it, if the right is your side or your lane is dying. Neither? Sail fast in what you have and wait for the fleet to spread, which it does within two minutes.

A tack is a decision, not a reaction. If you cannot say the reason out loud (“hole behind, going right”, “lane dying, clear air on port”) it is a panic tack, and the answer is no.

On the waterAfter your next poor start, do not tack in the first thirty seconds. At thirty, say the reason out loud before any tack. If you cannot say one, sail on. You’ve got it when your coach has not seen you tack in the first thirty seconds after a poor start, and you can give the reason for every tack you made in the first minute.

13.8Back to your plan

The first minute is tactics: speed, lanes, holes. The card is strategy: which side, and why. At one minute, with speed and a lane, the card takes over again. Read the after-the-gun line: hold, tack early, or wait for the shift.

If the start gave you your lane and your side, nothing changes: do what the card says. If the first minute took you somewhere else, a duck across to the right or a foot off to the left, reconnect: where am I now, where is my side from here, and what is the first move from here? The plan is the same; only the starting point moved.

Change the plan only for the reason on the card, the “change the plan if” line (chapter 9), or for a change in the wind you can see: more pressure on the other side, a shift that has settled. On a swinging day a header at one minute is a reason to tack; a flick that lasts ten seconds is not (chapter 4).

At two minutes, say where you are against the plan: “left, clear air, on plan” or “middle, lost the lane, going right now.” The sentence is the whole of this chapter in a few words, and it is the one your coach will ask for after the race.

On the waterAt one minute after the gun, say your first move out loud as it is on your card, and do it or say why not. At two minutes, say where you are against the plan in one sentence. You’ve got it when after the race you can say your one-minute and two-minute sentences, and your coach agrees that the beat you sailed is the plan on your card, or you can say where it left the plan and why.
