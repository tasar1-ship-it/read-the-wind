Every beat is full of meetings: a boat coming at you on the other tack, a group crossing your path, a boat about to tack on your wind. This chapter is about what to do at each one: your choices on starboard and on port, how to pick the smallest loss, when to tack in front of a group and when behind, how to protect your lane without sailing the wrong way, how to escape without losing twice, when to race the boat and when the course, and why a good choice can still lose.

16.1On starboard: hold, lee-bow or wave through

You are on starboard and a boat on port is coming at you. She has to keep clear of you S1: she will cross ahead if she can, duck behind you, or tack. You have three choices, and the first is the usual one.

Hold your course. Sail on, flat and fast, and let her solve it. Most port boats duck, and a boat that ducks you is a length behind you on the ladder. Keep an eye on her until her bow is clearly going behind your stern, and if she does not keep clear, turn away in good time; the rules about changing course and avoiding contact are chapter 18.

Tack on her, if you want to go right anyway and she would otherwise tack under you: a , finished a length ahead of her and just to leeward, puts her in your bent wind and makes her tack or fall back. It needs room: from head to wind until you are close-hauled on port you keep clear of her S5, and then you give her room to keep clear of you at first S5. Start early, about three or four lengths before you reach her, because she keeps sailing while you turn. Closer than that, hold your course. A doubtful gap is a reason not to.

, when her tacking under your bow would be worse for you than her crossing. Do it early, while she is still five or six lengths away and has not started to bear away for a duck: bear away a little, so that your bow points just behind where her stern will be, call “go on”, and let her pass ahead. You lose up to a length, about what a duck costs, and keep a clean lane. Once she is already ducking you, hold your course: a starboard boat that bears away then can break a rule and cause a collision S5 (chapter 18).

On the waterIn your next race, each time a port boat comes at you, say your choice out loud before she gets there: “hold”, “tack on her” or “wave her through”. You’ve got it when you say the choice before every meeting on starboard, and after the race you can say why you chose it each time.

16.2On port: cross, tack or duck

You are on port and a starboard boat is coming. You keep clear of her S1, and you have four choices. Decide early: look at her from ten lengths away and decide by five, because every one of them is worse when it is late. If you have not looked under the boom in the last minute, look now: your sail can hide her.

Cross and carry on, when you clearly cross her with room to spare and the lane ahead of you is good: the side you want, clear air. Cross and tack, when you cross her with two clear lengths (section 16.4) and a tack on her weather side leads towards better wind and leaves you in a clean lane; now she is behind you and to leeward.

Lee-bow, when you cannot quite cross and the new lane matters: a tack that finishes a length ahead of her and just to leeward, so that she is in your bent wind. To finish there you have to start early, about three or four lengths before you reach her, because she keeps sailing while you turn. Closer than that, duck. It needs enough room to finish the tack and give her room S5; a marginal gap, big waves or any doubt about the rules are reasons to choose something else.

, when the side you are on is still the side you want and ducking keeps your speed: decide early, bear away smoothly, pass close behind her stern and come back up. Look behind her first, so that you do not duck straight into the next boat. When you cannot cross, the duck is usually the cheapest of what is left, and the one sailors are most afraid of.

On the waterIn your next race, each time you meet a starboard boat on port, say your choice out loud five lengths away: “cross”, “cross and tack”, “lee-bow” or “duck”. You’ve got it when you say the choice five lengths away every time, and your coach has not seen you make a late turn in front of a starboard boat.

16.3Pick the smallest loss

Every meeting costs something. The skill is not avoiding the cost but picking the smallest one that keeps your plan. Put numbers on them.

A duck: about a length, your speed kept, your side kept. A tack you chose: a length or two, and you are on the other tack, which is fine if you wanted it. A tack you were forced into: a length or two, plus the wrong side, plus the leverage you did not want (chapter 15). A lee-bow that did not quite work: you are slow, head to wind, with a boat arriving. A collision or a penalty: turns, a protest, the race.

Seen like that, the answer is usually plain. A controlled duck of one length beats a forced tack, beats a doubtful lee-bow, and beats any risk of contact by a mile. The sailors who lose most at meetings are the ones who will not give up a length to keep the plan.

The exception is the meeting that changes the plan: a group crossing that reveals pressure you had not seen, a boat that tells you the shift has come. Then the smallest loss may be a tack, because the plan has moved. Chapter 14's three questions decide that.

On the waterAt your next meeting with another boat, say the cost of each choice out loud in lengths before you choose: “duck one, tack two and the wrong side”. Take the smallest that keeps the plan. You’ve got it when after the race you can say the choice you made at two meetings and what each cost in lengths, and you did not lose more than two lengths at any meeting.

16.4Tack in front or behind a group

A group of boats on starboard is coming across your path. Crossing one boat is a meeting; crossing a group is a decision about the whole beat, because where you end up decides your lane for minutes.

In front: tack onto starboard ahead of the group, if you cross the first boat with two clear lengths and the lane ahead of you is clean. Now you lead them, in clear air, with the group behind you. Less than two lengths and you finish the tack slow in the first boat's path, and either she avoids you or she rolls you.

Behind: duck the group and carry on, if the side you are on is the side you want. Or pass behind them and tack after the last boat, if the group is about to tack away and the lane they leave is clean. Never tack straight behind a group that is sailing on: you land in the dirty air and bent wind of every boat in it.

Say which, early, from five lengths: “in front, two lengths clear” or “behind, keep the side”. A group is never a surprise: you can see it coming for a minute, and the choice made at five lengths is cheap while the one made at one length is not.

On the waterWhen a group crosses your path in your next race, say “in front” or “behind” out loud five lengths away, with the reason. Tack in front only with two clear lengths. You’ve got it when after the race you can say what you did at each group and why, and you were not rolled by a group you tacked in front of.

16.5Protect your lane without going the wrong way

A boat is about to tack on your wind, or is climbing into your lane from below. The easy escape is to tack away. The easy escape is often the expensive one, because it takes you towards the side you did not choose, and when the shift you were waiting for comes, it pays the boats on your side, not you.

So before you tack, ask where the tack takes you. If it takes you towards your side, or along it, tack. If it takes you the wrong way, look for the small loss that keeps the side. off a length, when there is open water below you (chapter 13), to get out from under the boat that has tacked on your wind. Against the boat climbing from below, hold a few seconds in a slightly higher mode, never as a long pinch. Or, if you are on port, duck the next starboard boat and carry on in clean air on the same side.

A lane is worth protecting only while the side is worth it. If the side has stopped paying, the boat on your wind has done you a favour: tack, and go where the wind is.

The sailor to copy is the one who gives up a length to stay on the right side, not the one who gives up the side to keep the length. A ten degree shift on a hundred metres of leverage is seven or eight lengths (chapter 14); the lane is two.

On the waterIn your next race, when a boat threatens your lane, say out loud where a tack would take you, “my side” or “wrong way”, before you do anything. On “wrong way”, foot off or hold instead. You’ve got it when after the race you can say each time you kept your side with a small loss instead of tacking the wrong way, and your coach counted no tack away from your side without a reason.

16.6Escape without losing twice

When a lane is dying (chapter 13) you leave it, and leaving costs a length or two. The second loss is the one that is avoidable: tacking out of one shadow into another, or into the back of a queue, and paying all over again.

Look before you leave. The lane you are going to is in front of you: its boats, their shadows, the water. Is there a boat ahead and to windward of where you will end up? Is there a boat that will tack on you as soon as you settle? If the new lane is bad too, the right escape may be a duck or a foot off instead of a tack, or a tack a little later, when the lane is clear.

Then make the escape clean: one tack, flat and fast, speed first on the new tack (chapter 13), and no second tack for at least a minute unless the plan says so. Two tacks in a minute is two to four lengths gone before the wind has said anything.

The sentence is “out, and into what?” If the answer is “clear air, my side”, go. If it is “her shadow”, the boats on your tack sail on with you, so a few seconds change nothing. Change something: foot off first if there is water below, or wait until she has tacked away or pulled clear, and look again.

On the waterIn your next race, before every escape tack, say out loud what you are tacking into: “clear air” or the name of the boat whose shadow is there. Tack only on “clear air”, or wait and look again. You’ve got it when after the race you can say what each escape landed in, and none of them landed in a shadow you could have seen.

16.7Race the boat or race the course?

At every meeting there are two things you could be racing: the boat in front of you, or the course. Racing the boat means covering her, tacking on her, making her duck. Racing the course means sailing the fastest route through the wind and treating the boat as weather.

Most of the time, race the course. The fleet is seventy boats and the wind decides the order; a minute spent controlling one boat is a minute in which the shifts and the pressure are deciding your place among the other sixty-nine. The boat you beat by covering her is usually one boat; the lane you lost doing it costs five boats.

Race the boat when the series says so: the last race, one rival, the points between you decided by whether she is ahead or behind at the finish (chapter 15). Then cover her, tightly if you must, and let the others go.

The question is worth asking out loud at each meeting, because the body wants to race the boat: she is there, she is close, beating her feels like winning. The course is quieter, and it is where the race is.

On the waterAt your next three meetings, say out loud “the course” or “the boat” before you choose, and choose “the boat” only when you can name the series reason. You’ve got it when after the race you can say at which meetings you raced the course and at which the boat, and every “boat” had a reason from the series.

16.8A good choice can still lose

You ducked, kept your side, and the shift never came: three boats that tacked away beat you to the mark. Was the duck wrong? No. A good choice is one made well on what you knew, and a good choice loses some of the time. A bad choice wins some of the time too.

This matters because the lesson you take from a race depends on it. If you judge choices by their results, a lucky corner teaches you to go to corners, and a sound duck that lost teaches you to stop ducking. Both lessons are wrong, and both will cost you a series.

So after the race, ask two questions about each big choice, separately. Was it a good choice, given what I could see at the time? And did it work? “Good choice, did not work” is the most common answer in sailing, and it is a fine one. “Bad choice, worked” is the dangerous one.

The race card (chapters 21 and 23) has a line for this. Fill it in honestly, race after race, and the good choices will win over a season, because the odds add up. That is the whole of racing a big fleet: not winning every meeting, but choosing well at every one.

On the waterAfter your next race, for your two biggest choices, say two answers each: “good choice” or “bad choice”, and “worked” or “did not work”. You’ve got it when you can give both answers for two choices after every race, and at least once you have said “good choice, did not work” without changing the choice for next time.
