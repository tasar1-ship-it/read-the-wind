One picture explains most of upwind racing: the ladder. Once you can see it on the water, you will know why a header can be good news, why the lifted tack wins, why sailing far away from the other boats is a gamble, and why the corners are a trap. This chapter uses and from chapter 2, so if either word is new, tap it.

3.1What the rungs are

Imagine lines drawn across the course at right angles to the wind, like the of a ladder lying flat on the water. sits at the top. Every boat sailing upwind is climbing the ladder.

Two boats on the same rung are level. It does not matter how far apart they are or which tack they are on: they have the same amount of upwind still to sail, as long as neither has gone past a (section 3.5). If the wind stayed exactly the same, they would stay level.

So do not judge who is winning by who looks closer to the mark. A boat far out to the side can look a long way behind and still be level with you. Look along the rung instead: which boats are on your rung, which are above it, and which are below it.

On the waterOn the first beat, pick a boat well out to the side and say out loud whether it is above your rung or below it. You’ve got it when your call matches what happens when the two of you meet: the boat you called ahead crosses ahead, as long as the wind has not shifted in between.

3.2A shift turns the ladder

The rungs always lie across the wind. So when the wind changes direction, the whole ladder turns with it.

Watch the picture. The wind shifts to the right, and the rungs turn with it. The boat on the right is suddenly on a higher rung than the boat on the left. Nobody sailed a metre. The right-hand boat now has less upwind sailing left, and the left-hand boat has more.

That is the most important rule of the ladder: when the wind shifts, the boats on the side it shifted towards gain. Wind shifts right, the right side gains. Wind shifts left, the left side gains.

It is also why places change so fast on a . Two boats that were level a minute ago can be several boat lengths apart without either of them doing anything wrong.

On the waterEach time you feel a , look across at a boat well out to the side and say which of you just gained. You’ve got it when you make the call before the two of you cross, and the cross proves you right.

3.3Tack on the header

Now the part that feels backwards. Say you are out on the right on , and the wind shifts right. You feel it as a : your bow swings away from the mark. It feels bad. Here, it is good news. The wind has come to your side, and you have just gone up the ladder.

But if you keep going right, the gain is at risk: when the wind swings back, the ladder turns back and takes it away again. So . Now you are on the , pointing much closer to the mark, and sailing back towards the boat that was level with you while you are ahead of it. When that boat tacks towards you, it is on port and you are on starboard, so it must keep clear S1. And as long as the wind has not swung back first, you are ahead, so it has to pass behind you.

So on a day when the wind swings back and forth, the rule is short: sail the lifted tack, and tack on a real header, not a wobble. Chapter 4 shows the main kind of day when this rule is wrong.

On the waterSay “header” out loud when the bow swings away from the mark, count to five to check it stays, then tack. You’ve got it when after the tack your bow points closer to the mark than it did before the header.

3.4The further apart, the bigger the jump

How far a shift moves you up or down the ladder depends on how far apart you are sideways. Two boats side by side hardly change. Two boats on opposite sides of the course can swap places in one shift.

Here is an example with numbers. Two Optimists are level, 20 boat lengths apart. The wind shifts 10 degrees towards the right-hand boat. Now it is about three and a half boat lengths further up the ladder, without sailing a metre more. Put them 40 boat lengths apart and the same shift gives about seven. Twice as far apart, twice the jump.

It works both ways. If the wind shifts the other way, you lose exactly as much. That sideways distance from the other boats is called , and taking it is a bet: a big win if the wind comes your way, a big loss if it does not.

So when you are not sure what the wind will do, stay nearer the middle and near the boats you are racing. Take leverage only when you have a reason: more wind on that side, or a you have seen coming (chapter 4).

On the waterBefore you split away from the boats around you, say your reason out loud. You’ve got it when you can name the reason every time you split, and when you have no reason, you stay with the group.

3.5Never go to the corner

The is the line from which you can just reach the mark on one tack. Once you are on it, you have no choices left: a lift is wasted and a header hurts.

If you are lifted on the layline, you have sailed too far, or : you could have tacked earlier, and the extra metres are wasted. If you are headed, you can no longer reach the mark and need two more tacks, while the boats on the other side gained from that same shift.

Going past the layline into the corner is worse. Every metre past it takes you away from the mark, and you have to sail it back again. In the picture, grey and black sail together to the layline. Grey tacks and sails straight to the mark. Black keeps going into the corner and has to come all the way back.

The rule: tack for the mark as soon as you reach the layline. And stay inside the laylines until you are close to the mark, so the ladder can keep working for you on the way up (chapter 7).

On the waterAs you get near the layline, look across at the mark. Tack when it sits a little behind square to your boat, and a bit further behind in light wind or waves, because the boat slips sideways. After the tack, your bow should point just above the mark. You’ve got it when you reach the mark , without pinching to make it and without easing your sheet to reach down to it.
