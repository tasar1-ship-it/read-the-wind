14The beat in three parts

The first minute is over, you have speed and a lane, and a beat of ten or fifteen minutes lies ahead. This chapter divides it into three parts with different jobs: the lower third, where you get fast and free and head for your side; the middle third, where you compare yourself with the boats on your rung and decide what comes next; and the upper third, where you bank what you have gained and plan the last two tacks. Then two habits for the whole beat: the long tack when the picture is unclear, and a new look after every big change.

14.1Lower third: fast and free

The first third of the beat has three jobs, in this order: speed, clear air, and getting towards your side. The start and the first minute gave you the first two (chapter 13). The third is the plan on your card, and the lower third is where you connect to it.

How far out to go depends on how sure you are. Going out to a side is a bet whose size you choose. More wind you can see, or a one-way day you are sure of: go, with on the fleet, because the advantage pays for it. A guess: stay within a few lengths of the middle, where both sides are still open and a shift costs little.

When the mark is not straight upwind, sail the first (section 14.4). It points you more towards the mark and keeps you away from an early . On a swinging day tack on the real headers, checked against your heading, the boats beside you and the water (chapter 4), not on every flick.

When more wind is the whole story, go to it, by the shortest route that keeps you in clear air. In an Optimist more wind beats a small loss of angle (chapter 4), and the lower third is where the wind you spotted before the start is still where you left it.

On the waterIn the lower third of your next beat, say out loud how sure you are of your side, “sure” or “guess”, and go out wide only on “sure”. On “guess” stay within a few lengths of the middle. You’ve got it when after the race you can say which word you used and how far from the middle you went, and your coach saw the same from the coach boat.

14.2Middle third: compare on the ladder

In the middle third the question changes from “where am I going” to “is it working”. The way to know is to compare, and to compare with the right boats. Not the leader far ahead: the boats on your own , level with you across the wind. They share your wind, and if one of them gains on you, something real is happening.

Pick two or three boats on your rung, one on each side if you can, and watch them for a minute at a time. Both sides level: the wind is even, keep going. The boats on one side climbing: there is more pressure or a better angle there, and the next decision is about that side. All of them climbing on you: you are slow, and the next decision is about your boat, not the course.

The middle third is also where you meet the fleet coming back, and every meeting asks one question: what comes next? If you can cross a pack, ask whether they are already in the next shift or the next pressure. If they are, tack and take them back with you (chapter 15); if not, carry on into the advantage they have not used. If you cannot cross on port, ask whether you like your lane and your side. If you do, duck and carry on. Chapter 16 is the whole of that question.

Say your comparison out loud each minute: “left boats level, right boats up two lengths.” The sentence tells you where the wind is, and it is the one your coach wants after the race.

On the waterIn the middle third of your next beat, name two boats on your rung, one each side, and say each minute whether they are level, up or down on you. You’ve got it when after the race you can say which boats you watched and what they told you, and the side that was gaining is the side you went to.

14.3Upper third: bank your gains

In the upper third the gains you have made are worth more than any you might still make. Near the laylines the risk grows: choices disappear and the fleet gathers. So the job changes again: protect what you have, come back towards the middle or towards the starboard approach to the mark, and plan the last two tacks before the mark gets crowded.

The first job is to reduce leverage. If you are ahead of boats out to one side, come back across their bows and sit between them and the mark, so that a shift can move them only a little against you. If you are behind, the upper third is late for a big bet; a small one, a few lengths of separation towards the side that is gaining, is all the beat has left to give.

The second job is the four-fifths warning: while the mark is still some way off, when you are about four fifths of the way from the middle to a layline, stop and ask whether a strong reason remains to go on. Usually there is none, and you tack. The figure is a reminder to think, not a distance to measure.

The third job is the plan for the last two tacks: where you tack onto port and where you tack back to come to the mark on starboard, where the port-tack boats will be, and where the begins. Chapter 17 is the approach itself; the upper third is where you decide it, while there is still room to.

On the waterIn the upper third of your next beat, before you reach a layline, say “stop and ask” out loud and give your answer, and say your last two tacks before you make the first of them. You’ve got it when you reach the windward mark on starboard without overstanding, in most of your beats, and you can say where you decided the last two tacks.

14.4When the picture is unclear: the long tack

Some beats give you nothing to go on: no pattern in the wind, no dark water on either side, both sides of the fleet level. Then you need a rule that loses little whatever happens, and the rule is the long tack.

The long tack is the one on which the mark is more ahead of you, the one you have more of to sail (chapter 4). You find it by looking: on which tack does the mark sit closer to the bow? If the mark is straight upwind the two tacks are equal, and there is no long tack until the wind shifts.

Sail it first. It keeps you near the straight line to the mark and far from both laylines, so that a shift you could not predict costs little either way. And it keeps the short tack for the end, when you know more: the last few minutes of wind, the fleet, the mark itself.

The long tack is a rule for an unclear picture, not for every beat. When the wind is one-way, or there is more pressure on the short-tack side, the plan decides and the long tack waits. But when you are standing in the boat with nothing to choose on, choose the long tack, and keep looking.

On the waterAt the start of your next beat, say which tack is the long tack by looking at where the mark sits against your bow. When nothing else decides, sail it first. You’ve got it when you can name the long tack before every beat, and on the beats where nothing else decided, you sailed it first.

14.5Check your plan after every big change

A plan is only as good as the wind it was made in. Every big change means a new look, and the big changes are three: a shift you can see is settled, a band of pressure that has moved or arrived, and a cloud or a dying breeze that changes the whole day.

The look takes ten seconds and three questions. Is it a shift or a flick? A flick is gone in half a minute; a shift stays, and the boats around you show it too (chapter 4). Is my side still the side? With the new wind, is more pressure or the better angle still where I am going? Where am I on the beat? Lower third: plenty of time to change. Upper third: bank what you have.

Then say the answer: “shift, settled, left still the side, middle third: carry on,” or “pressure gone from the right, lower third: back to the middle.” The card's “change the plan if” line (chapter 9) does the same job for the start, written down before it; on the beat, a big change is the one reason to change the plan (chapter 8).

A plan kept out of pride after the wind has changed is the most expensive habit on the course. A plan changed every time the wind breathes is the second most. The three questions are the line between them.

On the waterIn your next race, every time the wind changes in a way you can see, stop and say the three answers out loud: shift or flick, side still the side, which third. You’ve got it when after the race you can list the big changes in the beat and what you said at each, and your coach counts the same number of changes.
