Chapter 7 gave you the seven answers. This chapter turns them into a side. It shows when the left really pays and when the right does, why the middle is so often the right answer, how to say how sure you are and what that decides, how to put the whole plan in one sentence, and how to choose Plan B and the one thing that switches to it. At the end of this chapter most of the plan half of your race card (chapter 21) is ready; chapter 9 adds the line and the start lines, and Part III takes you to the line.

8.1When the left pays

The left pays when something you can point to is on the left: more wind, seen as darker water that stayed through your count of 20 and boats heeling in it (chapter 1); a one-way shift turning left, so that the wind keeps moving towards the left and the left is the side to go to (chapter 4); current running with you on that side, or against you on the other (chapter 5); or the land bending the wind there: a gust lane off the land, the stronger wind round a point, or the shore effect you have learnt at your club (sections 1.5 and 4.5).

One of those is a clue. Two agreeing, seen more than once, is a side. A split test (chapter 7) that came back “left by three lengths” with a reason counts as seeing the side a second time. The first-beat manual's word is genuinely favoured: the left that keeps answering the same way, not the left that looked good once.

The left is the easy side to reach and the awkward side to come back from. After the gun every boat is on starboard, heading left, so from the pin end you hold your tack and nobody can make you tack (chapter 13). But you come back to the mark on port, keeping clear of every starboard boat S1, and the port approach is the one chapter 17 warns about. So a left plan has three parts: start near the pin, hold starboard in clear air, and tack back well before the port layline so that your last tack is onto starboard below the queue (section 17.4), not a port-tack arrival at the mark among starboard boats (section 17.5).

What the left is not: the corner. A left that pays is sailed as far as the wind that pays it, and no further (chapter 3).

On the waterBefore your next start, if your side is left, say the two clues that agree, out loud, and say where the left ends: “to the dark water, then back”. You’ve got it when you have gone left for a reason you could name, and you have come back to the mark on starboard with the mark still ahead, not on port into the starboard queue.

8.2When the right pays

The same clues, on the other side: more wind on the right that stayed; a one-way shift turning right; a current that helps on the right; a shore or a point on that side that bends the wind. And the same test: two clues agreeing, seen twice, and a split test that came back “right” with a reason.

The right has the opposite shape. It is harder to reach, because reaching it means tacking onto port early, through or behind the boats still on starboard (chapter 13: your first escape). It is easier to come back from, because you return to the mark on starboard, with right of way over the port boats S1, on the starboard approach that chapter 17 prefers (joined late, section 17.3).

So a right plan has its own three parts. Start near the committee boat, or wherever a lane will let you tack early: the start that gives immediate access to the right, in the coaching manual's words, is the boat end. From the boat end the hole is there at the gun, with nobody to cross; from anywhere else take the first hole that opens as the fleet spreads (chapter 13), at the cost of one duck at most. Then sail the right as far as the clue goes, and come back on starboard with the mark ahead.

The trap on the right is the same as on the left, with one more. Going right early on port means crossing the fleet, and a boat that has to duck ten boats to get there has paid for the side before it arrived. If the lane to the right costs more than the side is worth, the answer is the middle, with the right kept as the side you lean to (section 8.3).

On the waterBefore your next start, if your side is right, say the two clues that agree, say where your first escape onto port will be, and count the boats you expect to duck to get there. You’ve got it when you have gone right for a reason you could name, through an escape you chose, and come back to the mark on starboard.

8.3When the middle is best

When you cannot find two clues that agree, and many days are like that, the answer is the middle. The first-beat manual says it plainly: when the evidence is uncertain, staying near the centre of the course keeps your options and limits your leverage, and you leave the middle only for a strong enough reason: better pressure, a helping current, or a one-way shift you can rely on.

The middle is not doing nothing. It is the choice that loses least whatever the wind does, and on a swinging day it is where the shifts are won: you tack on the real headers, both laylines stay far away, and a shift either way moves you only a little against the boats around you (chapter 3). The coaching manual's plan for an uncertain day is three things: clear air, the long tack, the centre.

What the middle costs is the big gain. A side that pays by ten lengths pays the boats that went there, not the boats in the middle. That is the trade, and it is a good one whenever you cannot say why a side would pay.

The middle also has a start of its own: the middle of the line, often less crowded, with the sag that gives you a front row if you have a transit (chapter 10). From there both sides are open, and the long tack first (chapter 14) keeps them open for longer. Say it as a plan, not as a shrug: “middle, long tack first, lean left” if you have one clue, or no lean at all if you have none (section 8.4).

On the waterBefore your next start, when you cannot name two clues that agree, say “middle” out loud, and then say the long tack and the side you lean to. You’ve got it when you have chosen the middle on purpose, said why, and sailed a beat from it with a few lengths of leverage and both laylines open.

8.4How sure are you?

Every clue gets a grade. The first-beat manual grades the evidence strong, weak or none; the coaching manual says high, medium or low. This book says sure, half sure, not sure, and gives each one a test. Sure: two different clues agree and you have seen them more than once. Half sure: one clue, or clues you have seen only once. Not sure: no clue, or clues that disagree and the book cannot settle (section 4.6 for wind against angle, section 5.1 for current against a shift). When it does settle them, the clue that won counts as one clue: half sure. A one-way day from section 4.4 is sure when one of its two extra clues agrees with the readings. The test is the book's own; the three grades are the manuals'.

The grade is not decoration. It sets how far you go. Sure: go to the side, and take leverage on the fleet, because the odds are on your side. Half sure: lean, a few lengths from the middle towards the side, never to the layline. Not sure: the middle, the long tack, and stay connected to the boats around you. The coaching manual says it in one line: high confidence can justify separation; low confidence calls for the centre and the fleet. The grade sets how far you go in an ordinary race; chapter 15 adds the race you are in.

A sailor who goes to a corner on a half-sure clue has not chosen a big risk; the risk chose itself. A sailor who says “half sure, lean left” has chosen the size of the bet: risk deliberate, in the manual's words, rather than emotional.

Say the grade out loud before the start, and note it beside the card; the pocket card of section 21.5 asks it. After the race, check it: were you right when you said sure? Did you say not sure when you were? Chapter 23 keeps that count; over a season it tells you whether your eyes are getting better.

On the waterBefore your next start, say your side and your grade in two words, “left, sure”, “right, half sure”, “middle, not sure”, and then say how far that means you will go. You’ve got it when your grade matches what the wind did more often than not over a month, and your coach has seen you lean instead of commit on a half-sure day.

8.5Your one-sentence plan

The manuals end the reading with one sentence. The first-beat manual's is: start ___, protect ___, because ___. The coaching manual's is longer: the wind is mainly ___, the best feature is ___, I want to start ___ and protect ___. The illustrated guide: the best clue is ___, I want to start ___, my first move is ___, and how sure you are.

This book's sentence puts them together: “The best clue is more wind on the left, sure, so I start near the pin, hold starboard to the dark water, and protect the left.” Clue, grade, start, first tack, side; the illustrated guide calls the first tack the first move. Five things in one breath. On the race card (chapter 21) the clue goes on the shift and more-wind lines, the start on where I start, the side on the first-move line, the tack on the after-the-gun line from chapter 9, and the grade is said.

The sentence has a test built in. The coaching manual says that if you cannot say it clearly, the plan is not yet simple enough. A plan that needs three sentences has two plans in it, and in the last minute you will follow neither. Cut it until it fits, and what you cut is usually a clue you were not sure of.

Say it to someone: your coach, your partner from the split test, or the water if nobody is there. Said out loud it becomes something you can be wrong about, which is what makes the debrief (chapter 23) work. A plan that stayed in your head cannot be checked, and a plan that cannot be checked teaches nothing.

On the waterBefore your next start, say your one-sentence plan out loud to your coach or your partner: clue, grade, start, first tack, side. You’ve got it when you can say the sentence before every start without stopping to think, and after the race you can say which word in it was wrong.

8.6Plan A and the trigger for Plan B

Plan A is the sentence. is where you go when Plan A is cancelled, chosen before the start too, and it has two halves. For the start it is a place: your second place on the line from your start card (chapter 9), front row, the same side if you can still reach it. For the beat it is a side: the middle, the long tack first, a lean at most. A Plan B that is another corner is not a Plan B; it is a second bet.

Between them are the , one for each half of the plan: the one thing that cancels that half. Each is chosen while you are calm, and it is specific. The coaching manual's example is no clear lane at 45 seconds; the illustrated guide gives no usable space with 45 seconds left, and says the time is an example: practise your own. Your card's “change the plan if” line (chapter 9) is where you write the start trigger. The beat trigger is not written, because it is the same every race: the sign of section 21.2 and the new look of section 14.5.

The start trigger is about the place: for example no gap by one minute, or, if you fear the crowd more, a pile forming where you wanted to be; choose one. If it fires, you start from your second place, front row, with Plan A's side still the side if you can reach it. The beat trigger is about the wind, the sign that the day has changed: the tack that should be lifted is headed (chapter 21), or, on a pressure day, the pressure has gone from your side. If it fires, it is a new look (chapter 14) from Plan B's middle, and the lean is whichever side the look gives, or none. One trigger each, not a list: a sailor with five reasons to change the plan changes it at the first wobble.

On the waterBefore your next start, say Plan A, then “change if”, then Plan B, out loud, with one trigger for the start and one for the beat. You’ve got it when you have switched to Plan B at least once because the trigger fired and not because you were worried, and at least once kept Plan A through a wobble because it had not.
