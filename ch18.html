Every meeting in the last chapters had a rule under it. This chapter puts the handful you use on every beat in one place, in plain words: who keeps clear of whom on opposite tacks and on the same tack, what happens while you tack, the duties that come with right of way, and the five questions to ask before any close moment. It is not the rule book; the rule book decides. It is the part of it you need to sail a first beat in a big fleet without hitting anyone or being hit, and the way to pay at once if you get it wrong.

18.1Port and starboard

Your is named for the side the wind comes over: on starboard tack the wind comes over your right side and the boom is out to the left; on port tack the wind comes over your left side and the boom is out to the right. When boats on opposite tacks meet, the port-tack boat keeps clear of the starboard-tack boat S1. Every time, whoever was there first and whoever is faster.

means more than not hitting her. The starboard boat must be able to sail her course without having to do anything about you S7. If she has to bear away, head up or slow down because of you, you have not kept clear, even if nobody touched.

So on port the decision is yours and it is early: cross with a clear margin, tack, or duck behind her (chapter 16). On starboard the simplest job is to hold a steady course so that the port boat can plan around you; if you do change course you must give her room (section 18.4), and you still avoid contact if she gets it wrong S7.

The call “starboard” is a courtesy, not a rule: it tells the port boat you have seen her. Say it early and clearly, and do not expect it to make her disappear. If she does not react, you still avoid the collision, and you call “protest” straight away, which is a rule S7; your coach will show you the rest.

On the waterIn your next race, say your tack out loud at every meeting with another boat, “I am on port, she is on starboard” or the other way round, before you decide anything. You’ve got it when you name both tacks at every meeting without a pause, and your coach has not seen you surprised by a starboard boat.

18.2Windward, leeward, ahead and astern

On the same tack there are two cases. If the boats are , the boat keeps clear of the leeward boat S2. If they are not overlapped, the boat clear astern keeps clear of the boat clear ahead S7.

Overlap is the word that decides which rule you are in. Two boats are overlapped when neither is wholly behind the other. The moment any part of your boat, or anything fixed to it in its normal place, draws level with the very back of the boat ahead, her rudder and boom included, you are overlapped S6, and the windward one of the pair now keeps clear, whichever was ahead a second ago.

The leeward boat has the lane. She can sail her course and the windward boat has to go with her; that is why a lee-bow works, and why a boat climbing from below is a threat to your lane (chapter 13). Her right has limits. If she changes course she must give the windward boat room to keep clear S7. And there is a limit for a boat that came up from behind. If she got her overlap from clear astern, less than two lengths to leeward of the other boat, she may not sail higher than her proper course while they stay overlapped and that close S7. On a beat her proper course is close-hauled, the course she would sail with nobody there. A boat that tacked in under you is not limited that way, and before the gun there is no such limit S7. She must avoid contact like everyone else.

Clear astern means the boat behind looks after herself. If the boat ahead is slow, you go round her, not through her: you cannot sail into her stern and call it her fault. Anticipate, as you do in the starboard queue (chapter 17).

On the waterIn your next race, each time you are close to a boat on the same tack, say out loud “overlapped, I am windward” or “clear astern” or whichever it is, and what that makes you do. You’ve got it when you can say overlapped or clear, windward or leeward, at every close moment on the same tack, and you have not touched a boat you were keeping clear of.

18.3While you tack

A tack has a gap in the middle where you belong to nobody. From the moment you pass until you are on the new tack, you keep clear of every other boat S5. While you are turning you have no rights at all.

Before head to wind you are still on your old tack, with its rights. After you reach close-hauled the new tack's rights start, and if that gives you right of way you must at first give the other boat room to keep clear S5. The middle is yours to manage.

If two boats tack at the same time, the one on the other's port side, or the one behind, keeps clear S7. In a big fleet that happens at the start and at the laylines; know which one you are before you turn.

So a tack needs a place. Never start one that leaves another boat with nowhere to go while you are still turning: not under a starboard boat's bow or into a gap smaller than two clear lengths (chapter 16), unless it is a lee-bow started as early as section 16.2 says; and never in front of a boat already fetching the mark inside the zone (chapter 17). A tack made where there is room is a tack nobody can fault.

On the waterIn your next race, before every tack near other boats, say out loud “tack: clear” or “tack: wait”, and tack only on “clear”. You’ve got it when you have not made another boat alter course while you were tacking, and your coach agrees.

18.4Avoid contact, and give room when you change course

Right of way is not a licence. Three duties come with it.

First, everyone avoids contact if reasonably possible S7, right of way or not. A starboard boat that could still have turned away, and did not, has broken a rule too, and if the crash damages a boat or hurts someone she can be penalised as well S7. You hold your course so that the other boat can keep clear, and you keep watching her. If she has not started to turn by about three lengths, call to her and get ready. As soon as it is clear she will not keep clear, turn away in good time, usually bearing away behind her stern, and call “protest” straight away S7.

Second, when you change course with right of way, you give the other boat room to keep clear of the new course S7. A leeward boat that heads up sharply onto a windward boat that cannot get out of the way has broken this rule, whatever her rights. Turn so that the other boat can respond.

Third, when you get right of way by your own move, a tack onto starboard or a new overlap to leeward, you give the other boat room to keep clear at first S5. A lee-bow tack finished half a length from her bow gives you right of way you cannot use yet: she needs room to keep clear first, and if she had to turn before you were close-hauled you broke a rule already S5. The one time you need not give room at first is when the other boat's own move gave you the right of way, for example when she tacks onto port in front of you S5.

And one more limit, from chapter 16: on a beat, when a port boat is keeping clear by sailing to pass behind you, you do not bear away so that she suddenly has to change course S5. Hold your course and let her duck.

On the waterIn your next race, every time you have right of way and want to change course, say out loud “room” first, and turn slowly enough that the other boat can keep clear. You’ve got it when your coach has not seen a boat forced to make a sudden turn by a course change of yours, and you can say after the race where you held your course for a ducking boat.

18.5Your rules check

Before every close moment, the coaching manual gives five questions. Which tack am I on, and which is she? Are we overlapped or clear? Who has ? Is that right limited, because somebody is tacking, changing course, or ducking? And what is my safe way out if it goes wrong?

Said out loud they take three seconds: “port and starboard, I keep clear, duck”, or “same tack, overlapped, I am leeward, hold my course, give room”. The fifth answer matters most, because the rules decide who was wrong after the collision, and the safe way out is what stops it. One rule comes before all of these. If a person or a boat is in danger, you help, even in a race and even if it costs you the race S7. Stop racing, stay in your own boat, close enough to talk but not so close that your boat could hit them, call out, and wave to the coach or safety boat. Afterwards, ashore, ask your coach to help you fill in the form at the race office before the protest time ends: you can ask for a fair score for the race, and the protest committee decides S7. After any crash, look at the other sailor first and the rules second.

If you think you may have broken a rule about another boat, pay at once. Get well clear of other boats and do two full turns, each with a tack and a gybe, in the same direction S7. If you touch a mark, one turn S7. Taken promptly, the end the matter, unless you hurt someone, badly damaged a boat, or came out ahead anyway, far ahead of where you would have been: then the penalty is to retire S7. A protest you lose costs the race. While you do your turns, you keep clear of every boat that is not doing turns S7. Check the sailing instructions as well: at a few events they swap the turns for a different penalty S7.

That is the whole chapter: four rules of who keeps clear, three duties that come with right of way, five questions, and the turns. The protest room and the rest of the rule book can wait, apart from that first rule about helping anyone in danger; this is what you need on the beat.

On the waterAt your next three close meetings, say the five answers out loud before you act: tack, overlap, right of way, limit, way out. If you break a rule, do your turns at once and say what rule it was. You’ve got it when you can give the five answers in three seconds at any meeting, and every penalty you took in your last five races came as soon as you were clear of other boats, within seconds, not minutes.
