What the words mean

What the words mean

Every word with a dotted line in the book is here, in alphabetical order. Where the coach has a Spanish word for it, it is at the end of the entry.

Bear away

To turn the bow away from the wind.

At the club: arribar.

Beat

The upwind leg. You sail it in a zigzag because no boat can sail straight into the wind.

At the club: ceñida.

Chop

Short, steep waves close together, made by the wind blowing now. Longer, smoother waves that have travelled from far away are called swell.

Close-hauled

Sailing as close to the wind as you can while the sail still drives the boat. On the beat you sail close-hauled on port and starboard in turn.

At the club: ceñir, to sail close-hauled; rumbo de ceñida, a close-hauled course.

Committee boat

The anchored boat at one end of the start line, usually the right-hand end when you look upwind, from which the race officer runs the start. The other end is the pin.

At the club: barco del comité.

Corner

The far left or far right of the course, out at a layline or beyond it. From a corner you have one way to the mark and no choices left.

Corridor

A strip of the course running from the line to the mark: the left corridor, the middle, the right. In practice you sail each one on purpose, so that you learn what each side does before you have to choose one in a race.

Cover

Sailing so that a rival behind you cannot get past without passing through your position. A loose cover keeps you between the rival and the mark with your own clear air. A tight cover sits on one boat's wind and slows you both.

Current

Water that is moving as a whole, like a slow river, carrying everything that floats with it. Rivers and tides make it, and so does a wind that has blown across a bay for hours.

At the club: corriente.

Dirty air

Wind that another boat's sail has slowed and bent. It lies behind and to leeward of every boat, and it makes you slower and lower. Clear air is wind that no sail has touched.

At the club: aire sucio.

Duck

To bear away and pass behind the stern of a boat that has right of way, instead of crossing in front of her. A duck costs about a boat length.

Embat

The afternoon sea breeze in Mallorca. It fills in from the sea as the land heats up during the day.

At the club: embat.

Extension

The start line continued in a straight line beyond the pin or beyond the committee boat. Under the I flag, being over an extension in the last minute counts like being over the line.

Favoured end

The end of the start line that is further upwind. A boat starting there is higher up the ladder, closer to the mark along the wind, than a boat at the other end. How far ahead it is changes with every shift, and a big shift can move it to the other end.

Feather

A tiny turn towards the wind in a gust, once the boat is fast and flat, so that the sail carries a little less power and you climb a little. Tiny is the word: a big turn or a lot of rudder slows you down.

Fetch

To be able to pass to windward of the mark, leaving it on the side the course requires (in this book to port), without another tack. A boat on the layline fetches the mark; a boat below it does not, and has to tack twice more.

Foot

To sail a little lower than your best angle, with the sail full and the boat fast. Footing gives up a little height for speed, and for the room to get out from under a boat close to windward. Bearing away brings you closer to any boat to leeward, so check the water below you first.

Front row

The boats that are right on the line at the gun with nobody ahead of them. The second row starts a length or two behind, in their dirty air.

Gap

Space on the start line, usually just below you, that lets you bear away and speed up before the gun.

At the club: hueco.

General recall

When the race committee cannot tell which boats were over the line at the gun, usually because there were too many, or when it made a mistake in the starting signals, it can call the whole fleet back and start the race again. Chapter 12 explains the signal and what it means for a boat that was over.

Gust

A patch of stronger wind. It usually shows as darker, rougher water moving down the course.

At the club: racha.

Gust lane

A lane of stronger wind that stays in about the same place, usually where the wind pours through a gap in the land or round a point. Gusts keep coming down it for as long as the wind keeps its direction.

Head to wind

Pointing the bow straight into the wind, so that the sail flaps down the middle of the boat and the boat stops.

At the club: proa al viento.

Head up

To turn the bow towards the wind. Also called luffing.

At the club: orzar.

Header

A shift that pushes your bow lower, further from the wind's middle direction, on the tack you are on. In most of the course you see it as your bow swinging away from the mark. On a day when the wind swings back and forth, a real header means tack.

The same shift is a lift for the boats on the other tack.

Heading

The direction your bow points, as a number of degrees on the compass, from 0 to 360. Turn the bow to the right and the number gets bigger.

At the club: rumbo.

Heel

To lean over sideways. A gust makes a boat heel more, until the sailor hikes harder or eases the sheet.

At the club: escorar.

In irons

Stopped head to wind with the sail flapping and the boat not answering the tiller. It happens when you point too high for too long, or stall in a tack in strong wind. To get out, look behind you first. Then push the boom out to one side with your hand, so that the wind fills the sail from the wrong side and the boat moves backwards. Hold the tiller over to the same side as the boom. The stern swings away from that side and the bow swings towards it. When the bow has swung away from the wind and the wind comes over the side away from the boom, centre the tiller and sheet in. It costs lengths.

Individual recall

The signal that at least one boat was over the line at the gun: flag X on the committee boat with one sound. It does not say which boat.

Keep clear

What the boat without right of way has to do: stay out of the way so well that the other boat can sail her course without having to do anything, and, when the two are overlapped, can turn either way without touching you. Keeping clear is not avoiding a collision at the last second; it is leaving room.

Ladder rung

A rung is an imaginary line across the course, at right angles to the wind. The mark sits at the top of the ladder.

Boats on the same rung are level: they have the same amount of upwind still to sail, however far apart they are, as long as neither has sailed past a layline. When the wind changes direction the rungs turn with it, and that is what moves boats ahead or behind.

Lane

The strip of clear wind you sail in after the start, with room below you to bear away and room above you to point. The boats on either side of you make it or close it.

Layline

The line from which you can just reach the mark on one tack, sailing close-hauled. When you reach it, you tack once and sail straight to the mark.

Leading group

The bunch of boats that reaches the first mark first. Arriving in it is the real objective of the start and the first beat; winning the line is only a way of getting there, and not the only one.

Lee bow

A boat close ahead of you and to leeward. The wind leaving her sail reaches you bent, like a header, so you point lower and go slower. Being lee-bowed is the usual way a lane dies from below.

Lee-bow tack

A tack that ends with you just ahead of and to leeward of a boat you were meeting on the opposite tack, so that now, on the same tack, she is in your bent wind and has to tack or fall back. It needs room to finish the tack and give her room to keep clear.

Leech

The back edge of the sail, from the top back corner down to the end of the boom. The telltale on the top batten shows whether air is flowing off it or stalling behind it.

Leverage

How far apart you are sideways from another boat or group. More leverage means a bigger gain if the next shift comes your way, and a bigger loss if it goes the other way.

Lift

A shift that lets you point higher, closer to the wind's middle direction, on the tack you are on. In most of the course you see it as your bow swinging towards the mark. Keep going.

The same shift is a header for the boats on the other tack.

Lifted tack

The tack that points closer to the wind's middle direction right now. When the wind is to the right of its middle direction, it is starboard tack. When the wind is to the left of it, it is port tack. When your bow is lower than normal on one tack, the other tack is the lifted one.

Long tack

When the mark is not straight upwind, one tack points closer to it than the other, and you have to sail more of that tack to get there. That is the long tack. The other one is the short tack.

Lull

A patch of lighter wind, the opposite of a gust. It usually shows as smoother, paler water.

The mark

In this book, the mark always means the windward mark at the top of the first beat. In the pictures it sits right under the wind arrow.

At the club: boya, or boya de barlovento. The rule book calls it a baliza.

Mark-room

The room a boat is owed at a mark: room to sail to it and to round it without being squeezed out. Inside the zone, a boat that was overlapped on the inside when the first of them reached the zone, or clear ahead at that moment, is given mark-room by the other boat on the same tack. On a beat, boats on opposite tacks owe each other none.

Modes

Your gears upwind. Speed mode: a little lower, both luff telltales streaming, for acceleration. VMG mode: the normal fast angle; VMG is short for velocity made good, the part of your speed that takes you straight upwind, which is usually, not always, towards the windward mark; VMG mode is the angle where that part is biggest. Point mode: a little higher, for a lane. Pinch: higher still, only for a few seconds. You change gear on purpose, like a cyclist on a hill.

Normal

The direction your bow points, close-hauled on one tack, when the wind is in the middle of its swings. Higher than normal you are lifted. Lower than normal you are headed. Each tack has its own normal, and both move when the middle of the wind moves.

One-way shift

Wind that keeps turning the same way. The side it is turning towards wins, so go there first, even on a header. Coaches also call it a persistent shift.

At the club: rolada persistente.

Outhaul

The control at the back end of the boom that pulls the bottom back corner of the sail (the clew) out. Tighter makes the bottom of the sail flatter; looser makes it fuller.

Outside boat

The boat furthest out to one side of the fleet. She has the most leverage on everyone, so the next shift gains or loses her the most, and from a corner she has only one way to the mark.

At a mark the words mean something else: of two overlapped boats, the outside boat is the one further from the mark (section 17.7).

Over early

Being over the start line when the gun goes: part of your hull on the course side of the line. Chapter 12 explains the signals and what you have to do about it.

Overlap

Two boats are overlapped when neither is wholly behind the other: part of one boat is level with part of the other. A boat completely behind is clear astern, and the one in front is clear ahead. It is measured from a line across the very back of the boat ahead, rudder and boom included; two boats that are not level still count as overlapped if a boat between them is overlapped with both; and on a beat, boats on opposite tacks are never overlapped.

Overstand

To sail past the layline, so that after your last tack you have to ease the sheet and reach down to the mark. The extra distance is wasted.

Penalty turns

The way to pay for a rule about another boat that you may have broken, at once and on the water: two full turns in the same direction, each with a tack and a gybe, taken well clear of other boats; one turn for touching a mark. Taken promptly, they usually keep you in the race; section 18.5 says when they do not.

Pin end

The end of the start line marked by a buoy, opposite the committee boat.

At the club: boya de salida.

Pinch

To point higher than the boat wants, so that the front of the sail goes soft and the boat slows down. A little pinching for a few seconds can hold you up; a long pinch is slow.

Plan B

The plan you fall back on when Plan A is cancelled: a second place to start and a second side, chosen before the start, usually the middle. Chapter 8 shows how to choose it and the trigger that switches to it.

Port tack and starboard tack

You are on starboard tack when the wind comes over the right-hand side of your boat and your boom is out on the left. On port tack it is the other way round.

When two boats on opposite tacks meet, the boat on port must keep clear of the boat on starboard.

At the club: amurado a babor, amurado a estribor.

Pressure

The strength of the wind. More pressure means more wind. Sailors say “more pressure on the left” when there is more wind on the left of the course.

Race card

One small card for one race. Before the start you fill in what you see, your one first move and the start plan from chapter 9; after the race you fill in how the start went, one honest reason and one thing for next time; then you draw the first beat. Chapter 21 shows it; chapter 23 keeps the score.

Right of way

The boat the other one has to keep clear of. Starboard over port, leeward over windward, clear ahead over clear astern. It comes with duties: avoid contact if you reasonably can, and give room when you change course.

Runway

The gap below you, a length or two, seen as the space you need: open water to bear away into and reach full speed before the gun, with about three boat lengths of sailing to the line.

Sag

When the boats in the middle of a long start line hang back behind the real line, because nobody there can see the ends. The front row in the middle can be two or three boat lengths behind the line without knowing it.

Scorecard

A sheet with eight parts of a first beat, each scored 0, 1 or 2 after a race: the look before the start, the start plan, the start itself, lanes, shifts and pressure, risk, the layline and the mark, and the rules. It scores how you raced, not where you finished. Chapter 23 shows it.

Skew

A course is skewed when the first mark is not straight upwind of the start line but off to one side. Then one tack points closer to the mark than the other and you sail more of it: that is the long tack. Section 7.4 shows how to see the skew before the start.

Split test

A test before the start with a partner of about your speed: from one point you sail off on opposite tacks for the same few minutes, both tack back, and whoever crosses ahead sailed through the better wind or water. Section 7.3 shows how to do it and what it can and cannot tell you.

Sprit

The pole that runs from the mast up to the top back corner of the sail (the peak) and holds it up. More sprit tension pulls the peak out and changes the shape of the top of the sail; how much to use depends on your sail, so follow its maker's guide.

Strategy

Your plan for the beat, made from the wind, the water and the course: which side, which tack first, how far from the middle. The side it picks would be the same if you were the only boat on the course; the fleet shapes how far from the middle you go. Chapter 6 separates it from tactics, and chapters 7 and 8 show how to make it.

Swinging shift

Wind that swings left, then right, then left again, around its average direction. Tack on the headers. Coaches also call it an oscillating shift.

At the club: rolada oscilante.

Tack

To turn the bow through the wind, from port tack to starboard tack or back again.

At the club: virar.

Tactics

What you do about the boats near you while you carry out the plan: cross, tack or duck, lee-bow, hold a lane or leave it, cover. Tactics serve the strategy; a tactical move that gives away the plan is a loss even when it wins the meeting. Chapters 13, 15 and 16 are the tactics chapters.

Telltales

The small strips of wool or tape on your sail. They show how the air flows over it. When they stream back on both sides, the sail is working.

Transit

Two things lined up, such as the pin end and a building on shore behind it. When they line up, you know you are on the start line.

At the club: enfilación.

The triangle

The area of water with the pin, the committee boat and the first mark at its corners. The Z, U and black flags keep you out of it in the last minute before the gun.

Trigger

The one thing that, if it happens, cancels Plan A and starts Plan B: no gap at one minute, or the sign that the day has changed. It is chosen and said out loud before the start, so that the change is a decision and not a panic.

Vang

The control that holds the boom down. It keeps the back edge of the sail from opening when you ease the sheet, so it matters most in strong wind and in gusts. Some sailors call it the kicker.

Warning signal

The flag and sound that begin the starting sequence, normally five minutes before the gun. Your watch starts here, and the flags, not your guess, give the time.

Wave through

On starboard, letting a port-tack boat cross ahead of you instead of making her tack or duck: you bear away a little and call to her, early, before she has started to duck. You do it when her tacking under your bow would be worse for you than her crossing.

Wind shadow

The area of light, patchy wind behind something that blocks the wind: high land, buildings, trees or a big boat. A sail makes one too, and that one is called dirty air.

Wind shift

A change in the direction the wind comes from. A shift to the right means the wind now comes from further right as you look up the course towards the mark.

At the club: rolada. “El viento rola” means the wind is shifting.

Windward and leeward

Windward is the side the wind comes from. Leeward is the side it blows towards. A boat to windward of you is between you and the wind.

At the club: barlovento, sotavento.

Zone

The water within three boat lengths of a mark, where the mark rules begin to apply. Chapter 17 explains what changes when you enter it.
