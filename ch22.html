The habits in this book are built on the water, in sessions that each practise one thing. This chapter is the training plan: how to practise so that it sticks, then the sessions for reading the course, for the start, for the beat, and for gusts, each with what it measures. Take them to your coach; most of them come from the coaching manuals the book is built from, and your coach will have versions of their own.

22.1How to practise

Four rules make a session worth having. Before it, choose one chapter and one behaviour you can count: one, not ten. On the water, look and decide before the coach gives an answer. After each go, four questions: what did you see, what did you expect, what happened, what will you change next time.

The behaviour must be something your coach can see from the coach boat and you can count afterwards. Looks upwind in a beat. Tacks on real headers. Seconds from a dying lane to the escape. Front-row starts out of five. A number goes up or down; a feeling does not.

Your coach asks instead of telling, and on the water the coach's words are two questions: what told you that? which option did you keep? That is on purpose. The answer you find yourself is the one you will have in the race, when the coach boat is outside the course and cannot help you, unless you are hurt or in danger: then anyone may help S7.

The fourth is for after racing: judge the quality of the decision apart from the result, as chapter 16 says, because a good decision can lose when the wind does something unlikely. The sessions below each say what to count; chapter 23 keeps the score.

On the waterBefore your next session, say to your coach the one chapter and the one countable behaviour you are working on, and after each go answer the four questions out loud before the coach says anything. You’ve got it when you can name the behaviour and its count for each of the last four sessions, and the count went the right way in most of them.

22.2Sessions for reading the course

The first session is the three : left, middle, right, sailed one after the other up the same beat, each talked over with your coach before the next, then a free race. You sail every corridor before you choose, and before the free race you say which corridor will win and how sure you are. Afterwards: which cue was the most reliable, your heading, the colour of the water, the other boats, or the shore?

The second is shifts: sail ten boat lengths steady on starboard and note your heading, then on port, then both again, to find your normal headings (chapter 2), then a short race on a swinging course. The count is shift calls that were right against calls that were wrong, and how long you took from a header that had lasted long enough to be real (section 2.4) to your tack.

The first-beat manual names a third session, pressure and the long tack, but does not describe it. Ask your coach for a beat with a visible band of pressure on one side and a mark set off to one side, so that one tack is longer, sailed for the connection between pressure, long tack and the fleet (chapters 1, 4 and 14). A count could be how often you were in the pressure, and whether you sailed the long tack first.

All three start from the same question: where is the wind, and what is it doing? The sessions give you the course three times, the shifts in numbers, and the pressure in a band you can see, so that in a race you can see the same things faster.

On the waterAsk your coach for the three-corridor session, say which corridor will win before the free race and how sure you are, and after it say which cue was the most reliable. You’ve got it when your corridor call before the free race is right more often than not over a month, and you can say each time which cue told you.

22.3Sessions for the start

The start session is short starts, each followed by a finish a third of the way up the beat, so that the start is judged by where it put you, not by the gun. Your coach changes the line's bias between starts, or puts a visible pressure cue on one side, so that the right place on the line moves.

The counts are four: front-row starts out of the total, at full speed at the gun or not, your lane twenty seconds later, and whether your first decision matched your plan. The question afterwards is the one from chapter 9: was the start chosen for the bias alone, or for the beat?

The second start session is the first minute: starts followed by a short beat, repeated, with the decision in the first minute, hold or escape, as the thing measured (chapter 13). If your coach has video, watch the first minute back; one thing worth counting is the seconds between the lane dying and your move.

Both sessions are about the connection the book keeps coming back to: the start is not an end in itself. A front-row start that leads nowhere can finish behind a second-row start with a lane to the side you chose, and the finish a third of the way up the beat is what shows it.

On the waterAsk your coach for a short-start session with a finish a third of the way up the beat, and after each start say your four counts: front row or not, speed at the gun, lane at twenty seconds, plan followed or not. You’ve got it when over a session your front-row count is rising, your lane at twenty seconds is clean more often than not, and your coach agrees that you chose the starts for the beat.

22.4Sessions for the beat

Four sessions build the beat, each with its own counts. Modes and lanes: two boats sail side by side, the leeward one changing gear on the coach's signal, from speed to VMG to point, while the windward one reports the gain or loss; the counts are the time to full speed after a tack and how far you held a lane before it died (chapters 13 and 19).

Crossing decisions: pairs practise cross and carry on, cross and tack, lee-bow and duck in a controlled meeting, then the same choices inside a short fleet race, with scenario races on the port-and-starboard, tacking and changing-course rules (rules 10, 13 and 16, chapter 18); the counts are the speed you kept five lengths after the meeting and the lane you ended up in (chapters 16 and 18).

The late layline: every boat sails the early layline, the late layline and the port approach in turn before choosing freely; the counts are distance sailed, forced manoeuvres, speed entering the zone and places gained or lost (chapter 17).

And the whole thing: a full start-to-mark decision race, with a debrief by the sailors rather than the coach, each explaining how pressure, shifts and position changed the result. The measure is the one that chapter 23 keeps: five questions, answered with evidence.

On the waterAsk your coach for one of the four beat sessions and say its counts out loud at the end of each go; in the decision race, debrief with the other sailors before the coach speaks. You’ve got it when you can say the counts for each beat session you have done, and in the decision race you explained the beat to the others with evidence, not with results.

22.5A session for gusts

The gust session is sailed in gusty water on a reach that turns into a beat, so that some gusts arrive just as you sheet in and head up from the reach, when the boat is loading up anyway. The job is the first four stages of chapter 20: flatten with the sheet and the body first, then add the tiny feather, then trim back smoothly.

Three counts, and your coach watches for all three: the most the boat heeled in the gust, how much your heading changed, and how much speed you lost. A good gust is a small number for all three; a gust where the boat heeled hard, the bow swung and the boat slowed is the one to sail again.

Add the stages one at a time, as chapter 20 says. On the first day your coach asks only for the flat boat; the three counts are still taken. When that is a habit, hike before heel; then the feather; then the exit. The fifth stage, speed to height, is for the day your coach says the other four have become habits you no longer think about.

This is the only session that needs real gusts, so ask for it whenever the forecast is gusty, and write the three counts in your notebook, as chapter 23 says.

On the waterOn your next gusty day, ask your coach for the reach-to-beat gust session and, after each gust, say your three counts: heel, heading change, speed lost. You’ve got it when your coach has seen your three counts come down over a session, and you can say which stage of the gust you are on.
