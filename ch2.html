The wind is never steady. It swings a little to the left and a little to the right all day long, and every swing makes one tack better and the other one worse. This chapter teaches you to catch those swings: with your bow, with the other boats, and with a far-away point on the shore. Chapter 3 shows what to do with them.

2.1What a wind shift is

The wind hardly ever blows from exactly the same direction for long. It swings a few degrees one way, then a few degrees back. Each change of direction is a . Left and right always mean as you look up the course towards : after a shift to the right, the wind comes from further right.

Your boat is tied to the wind. , you point about 45 degrees away from it: to one side on , to the other side on port. So when the wind turns, your course turns with it, by the same amount.

That is why one shift does opposite things to the two tacks. Watch the picture. The wind shifts right, and both boats turn right with it. The boat on starboard swings its bow towards the mark. It can point higher: that is a . The boat on port swings its bow away from the mark. It has to point lower: that is a .

So a shift is never simply good or bad. It is good on one tack and bad on the other. The tack that has the lift is called the , and upwind racing starts with knowing which one it is right now.

On the waterSail close-hauled on starboard for two minutes, steering by your . Each time the wind lets you head up, say “up” out loud. Each time it makes you bear away, say “down”. You’ve got it when you can add which way the wind went. On starboard, up means it went right and down means it went left. Then try port, where it is the other way round.

2.2Watch your bow against the mark

You do not need an instrument to see a shift. You need your bow and one fixed point. Here the fixed point is . If you cannot see it yet, use something far away on the shore behind it.

Sail close-hauled and steer by your , so that the boat always points as high as the wind allows. Then look where your bow points. When the wind shifts, the bow swings. Bow swings towards the mark: , keep going. Bow swings away from the mark: , and a decision to make (section 2.4).

You usually feel a header before you see it. The front of the sail starts to flutter, and you have to to fill it again. A lift is quieter. The sail stays full, but the telltale on the far side of the sail stops streaming. until both telltales stream again. If you cannot see that telltale, let the bow come up gently until the front of the sail just flutters, then come back a little.

One warning. As you sail up the , the mark slides slowly sideways off your bow anyway, because you are sailing past it. That slow slide is not a shift. Your bow against the mark catches the quick swings. A slow, steady change hides in the slide. Section 2.5 shows how to catch it.

On the waterOn every beat, say “lift” or “header” out loud each time your bow swings. Every time. You’ve got it when you call it within a few seconds of the swing, and after each “header” you really did have to bear away to keep the sail full.

2.3Let the other boats tell you

Every boat on the beat is a wind arrow. A boat sailing close-hauled points about 45 degrees from its wind, so its bow shows you where the wind is coming from at the place where that boat is.

Look first at the boats ahead of you, further up the course, on your tack. The new wind reaches them before it reaches you. If their bows swing towards the mark, a lift is on its way to you. If their bows swing away, a header is coming. You get a few seconds of warning, and that is enough to be ready.

Then look at the boats beside you on your tack. If they turn at the same moment as you, the wind really has changed. If only you turn, it has not (section 2.4).

Boats on the other tack show the same shift the other way round, because your header is their lift. When a boat coming across on the other tack starts to point higher, you are probably being headed.

One warning: a boat only tells the truth when it is sailing well. A boat that is going slowly, or sitting in another boat's , points the wrong way. Read the fast boats in clear wind.

On the waterOn each beat, pick a fast boat on your tack about ten boat lengths further up the course and watch its bow. Each time it swings, say “lift coming” or “header coming”. You’ve got it when the same swing reaches your own bow a few seconds after you called it.

2.4Real shift or just a wobble?

Your bow swings away from the mark and you call “header”. Before you , make three quick checks.

Does it stay? Count to five. A swing that is gone before you reach five is not worth a tack. If your bow is back where it was, it was a wobble.

Are you still fast? A drop in the wind feels like a header. In a , or after a big wave, the boat slows and the telltales stop flying properly, but the wind has not changed direction. Get your speed back, then look again.

Is it only you? If the boats beside you have turned too, the wind has changed. If only you point low, a sail close ahead or just to is probably giving you its . Get to clear wind, even with a tack, but it was not a shift.

All three passed? The wind has shifted. Now compare with (section 2.5). If a lift has only gone away, stay. If your bow is clearly lower than normal, it is a real header: on a day when the wind , tack as soon as you have room (chapter 3).

Two cases wait for chapter 4. A header that comes with a : tack only if the new tack keeps you in the stronger wind. One side of the course that keeps gaining: it has more wind, or a bend in the wind, so choose a side, not a tack.

On the waterEach time your bow swings away, count to five out loud before you decide anything. You’ve got it when you sail a whole beat without tacking on a swing that had already gone by the time you reached five.

2.5Find your normal on each tack

“Lift” and “header” tell you which way your bow just swung. To know where you are, you need a : the direction your bow points on each tack when the wind is in the middle of its swings. Higher than normal, you are lifted. Lower, you are headed.

Before the start, near the start line, sail close-hauled on starboard for about a minute. Watch what your bow points at on the far shore: a hill or a tower. It must be at least ten times as far away as the mark: anything nearer slides along the shore. Do the same on port, then both again: one look is a clue, not proof. Your bow wanders with the wind. Halfway between its furthest left and furthest right is your normal: one point for each tack.

On the beat, find it again. On starboard, bow to the right of your point means lifted and bow to the left means headed. On port it is the other way round.

No far-away land? Then remember your last call. A swing away after a lift usually just takes the lift back: you are near normal, so stay. A swing away from normal is a real header. This way is rough: a slow, steady change still hides from it.

A compass does the same job with a number for each tack, and needs no land: sailors write down their two normal before the start. Even then, only the difference from normal counts.

On the waterShortly before each start, near the start line, sail each tack twice for about a minute, fast and in clear wind. Pick the far-away point in the middle of where your bow wanders: that is your normal for that tack. You’ve got it when on the beat you call out each change: “lifted”, “normal” or “headed”. After a tack on “headed”, your first call on the new tack is “lifted”.
