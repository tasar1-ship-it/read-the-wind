Chapter 6 said that every decision is read from five things. This chapter is about reading them before the start, in the twenty minutes that decide the beat: why you arrive early and what you do with the time, how to sail both sides and compare them, how a partner turns the comparison into a test, how to find the mark and the skew of the course and so the long tack, and what the fleet can tell you before the first gun. Chapter 8 then turns what you have read into a side.

7.1Arrive early

The coaching manual says it in one line: test the race area before the warning signal, rather than learning it during the race. The race committee sets the course from the same things, the wind's middle direction, the current, the favoured side and the shifts it expects; you are reading the same water from the boat.

So be in the race area twenty minutes or more before the first warning signal, half an hour when you can, and use the time in an order. When time is short, drop the second side and the split test before you drop anything else. The race area first, the line last. On the way out, take a first look at your normal on each tack, which you will fix near the line (chapter 2), and look at the whole course: dark water, the boats already up there, the shore. Sail both sides as far as time allows (section 7.2), and test the current at a mark on the way (chapter 5). Then, near the line, sail each tack for a minute, three times over, which fixes your normals and names the kind of day (chapter 4). Find the mark and the long tack (section 7.4). Watch the fleet (section 7.5).

Only then the line: the head-to-wind test at about ten minutes before the start, at six and after the five-minute signal, and your transit (chapters 9 and 10). The line changes until the gun, so the last look is the one that counts; the race area changes more slowly and needs the longer look.

The manual calls this the reconnaissance loop, and each test in it produces a decision: your normals, the kind of day, where the wind is and how reliable it is, the current, the favoured end, the long tack, where the fleet will crowd. By the warning signal all seven are said out loud, and the ones the race card asks for are on it (chapter 21).

On the waterAt your next regatta, be in the race area twenty minutes before the first warning signal and do the loop in order: a first look at your normals, the course, the current, the kind of day, the mark, the fleet, and the line last. You’ve got it when you can say your seven answers before the warning signal, and the line was the last thing you looked at, not the first.

7.2Sail both sides

You cannot read a side from the line. The water on the left may look darker from a distance and be nothing when you get there; the shift that pays on the right only shows itself to a boat sailing there. So sail both sides before the start: up the left side on one tack for a few minutes, back, up the right on the other, as far as the time allows.

On each side, three things. Pressure: is the water darker and rougher here than in the middle, and does the boat feel it, more heel, more speed? Chapter 1 showed that dark water alone can mislead you; the boats around you heeling and the feel of your own boat are the check. Angle: is your bow higher or lower than your normal on this side? Current: near a mark or a moored boat, is the water moving, and is it the same as on the other side (chapter 5)?

Compare, and say the difference out loud: “more wind left, same angle, no current”. What you are looking for is not a feeling but a difference you could point to. If you cannot name one, there may be none, and that is an answer too: it sends you to the middle (chapter 8).

The coaching manuals train this as the corridor exercise, left, middle and right sailed in turn before a free race (chapter 22). Before a start there is no time to sail all three, so the middle is read on the way out and back: compare what you saw there with the two sides. One short sail is a clue, not proof: the fleet (section 7.5) is a second look, and with a partner the split test (section 7.3) does this job better.

On the waterBefore your next start, sail a few minutes up each side of the course and say for each one: pressure, angle, current. Then say the one difference between the sides that you could point to. You’ve got it when you can name a difference between the sides before most starts, or say honestly that there was none, and your coach agrees with what you named.

7.3The split test with a partner

The best way to compare the sides is to be on both at once, and for that you need a partner of about your own speed. Two boats sample both sides at the same minutes, which one boat sailing up each side in turn cannot do, and with a partner the test takes the place of sailing both sides alone.

The : start level with each other at one point below the course. One of you sails off on starboard, up and to the left; the other on port, up and to the right. Sail flat out for the same time, three or four minutes, then both tack and sail back towards each other. Where you meet, one boat crosses ahead, or you are level, and level means the sides were the same. The boat that crosses ahead sailed through the better wind or water on its side: more pressure, a better angle, or a helping current.

Say what you found, with a size: “right by two lengths”. Then say why, if you can: the boat on the right saw darker water, or was lifted all the way out. The size says how much the side is worth; the reason says whether it will still be there after the gun.

What the test cannot tell you is what happens next. It measures those four minutes. On a swinging day the side that won the test usually loses the next swing, so a split test on a swinging day only tells you where the wind was; on a one-way day, or a day when more wind is the whole story, it tells you where the wind is going. Read the result with the kind of day you named (chapter 4). And agree the time before you start, and tack at it whatever the wind is doing: a boat that tacks on a header has used a shift, and the test then measures the tack, not the side.

On the waterBefore your next start at a regatta, do one split test with a partner of your speed, three or four minutes each way, and say the result with a size and a reason. You’ve got it when you have done the test at three regattas, and at least once you have said that the result meant little because the day was a swinging one.

7.4The mark, the skew and the long tack

Find the first mark before the start. It is often far away and small, so use the course board on the committee boat, look for the mark boat, and line the mark up with something on the shore behind it from near where you will start, so that you can find it again.

Then ask whether it is straight upwind. Head to wind, the mark straight over your bow means the course is square; if it sits to the right of your bow, port is the long tack, and to the left, starboard. Or sail close-hauled on each tack and see on which one the mark sits closer to the bow. If it sits closer on one tack, the course has a , and that tack is the long tack: the one you will sail more of to reach the mark.

The skew matters for two reasons. It makes one layline close and the other far: the long tack's layline runs back over the course, so a boat that takes the short tack first reaches it with most of the beat still to sail. And it decides the first tack when nothing else does: chapter 14 says sail the long tack first when the picture is unclear, and the picture is often unclear in the first minute.

The skew is not fixed. A shift moves straight upwind, not the mark, so the mark sits differently against it: a right shift makes the mark sit more to the left of straight upwind and lengthens the starboard tack; a left shift does the opposite. So on a one-way day the long tack can change during the beat, and a course that was square at the warning signal may be skewed at the gun. Check it with the last head-to-wind test, and say it out loud, as the pocket card of section 21.5 asks: “mark to the right, port is the long tack”.

On the waterBefore your next start, find the mark, line it up with the shore, and say which tack is the long tack from how the mark sits against your bow on each tack, then say it again after the last head-to-wind test. You’ve got it when you can point at the mark from the line without searching for it, and you can say the long tack before every start and whether it changed.

7.5Watch the fleet

The last thing to read before the start is the fleet. Seventy boats are seventy pairs of eyes, and before the start many of them have been where you have not. The coaching manual asks three things of the fleet: where are the good sailors practising, where are groups collecting, and, in the start before yours, did the middle of the line sag?

The good sailors first. Watch the two or three you know are fast: which side did they sail up, and how soon did they come back? Straight back may mean nothing there, or a header they tacked on; a group that stays out on one side has usually felt something, a shift or pressure. It is a clue, not an order, and you check it against your own look.

Then the crowd. Where groups collect before the start is where they will start, and there the air will be dirty and the lanes short: the favoured end draws a pile (chapter 10), and the side the fleet likes draws a second one. Reading the fleet means taking its clue and planning around its weight.

Then name the day, as the manual does, with the plan each kind brings: swinging, stay connected to the middle and tack on the settled headers; one-way, reach the side early but keep a way out; pressure, sail to the wind even at a small cost in angle; one side always paying because of the shore or the current, use it but keep checking; nothing repeatable, which chapter 4 calls not sure: clear air, the long tack and the middle. The first two and the last are chapter 4's three words; the other two are days when direction is not the main thing, section 4.6's more wind wins and section 4.5's one side keeps winning, and on the card both go on the more-wind line. That naming is the last line of the loop, and chapter 8 begins from it.

On the waterBefore your next start, name two fast sailors and say which side each one sailed up and came back from, say where the crowd is collecting, and then name the day in chapter 4's word, and say if it is a pressure day or a shore day as well. You’ve got it when you can say before the warning signal where the fast sailors went, where the pile will be, and which of the five kinds of day it is, and after the race two of the three were right.
