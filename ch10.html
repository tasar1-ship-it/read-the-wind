Chapter 9 chose the end and the side. This chapter is about the fleet: seventy boats on one line, and how to start well among them. It shows why the middle of a long line sags behind the real line and how a transit tells you where the line is, where the crowd will go and how to stay out of its piles, how to choose your lane early, and how to decide, before the start and not after it, what your first move after the gun will be.

10.1Where is the line? The middle sags

In a fleet of seventy boats the line is long, and from the middle you cannot see either end properly. The is a small buoy behind a wall of sails. The is a flag above more sails. So the boats in the middle judge the line by the boats beside them, and those boats are doing the same.

That is the . A boat that is afraid of being over hangs back half a length. Its neighbour lines up on its bow and hangs back a little more. By the gun the middle of the is two or three boat lengths behind the real line, and nobody in it knows. The boats at the two ends, which can see the end beside them, are on the line.

Two lengths at the gun is a lot. Add the minute of the row behind you gets, and it is six. The boat that starts on the real line, ahead of the sagging row, has clear air from the first second. The middle is where this book sends you when neither side is sure, so the sag is often your problem.

The answer is to know where the line really is without looking at the boats beside you. That is the , in the next section. With it you can be on the line at the gun, two lengths ahead of the row, and start those two lengths ahead of forty boats that never saw it.

On the waterIn the first minute after your next three starts, once you are at full speed, glance back along the line to the pin and to the committee boat, and say out loud where you were at the gun: on the line, behind it, or in a middle that sagged. You’ve got it when after each start you can say in boat lengths how far behind the line the middle was, and your coach, who watched the start from the coach boat, agrees.

10.2Your transit

A is two things in a line. For the start line, the two things are the far end of the line and something on the shore behind it: a house, a tower, a tree, a crane. When the far end and your shore mark line up, you are on the line, wherever along it you are.

Take it before the warning signal. Sail to one end, sit just outside it on the line, and look along the line past the other end to the shore. Whatever sits exactly behind that end is your transit. Say it out loud: “committee boat, pin, white house.” If there is shore behind the committee boat as well, take a second transit the other way, from the pin end. Two are better than one.

Then use it. In the last minute, look along the line at your far end. Is the house behind it? You are on the line. Does the end sit on the upwind side of the house, above it in the picture? You are behind the line, and the wider the gap, the further behind. Does it sit on the downwind side, below the house in the picture? You are over.

A transit only works while nothing moves. If the committee moves the pin, or the committee boat swings on its anchor, take it again. In open water with no shore there is no transit, only the two ends and your judgement: then start near an end you can see.

On the waterBefore the warning signal, sail to the committee boat, sit just outside it on the line, line up the pin with the shore behind it, and name your transit out loud. In the last minute, check it twice. You’ve got it when you can name your transit before every start, and at the gun you are on the line, not level with the boats beside you.

10.3Where will the crowd go?

Before you choose your place, guess where everyone else will be. The crowd goes to three places: the , because everyone did the head-to-wind test; the committee boat, because from there you can see the line and the flags and tack early to the right; and wherever the best sailors set up, because the others follow them.

On a long line in a big fleet there is not room for every boat in the front row, so wherever the crowd piles up, somewhere else is thin. The thin place is where the front row is cheap: a below you, clear air, and nobody over early beside you.

Look at three minutes, and again at two. Sail along behind the row and say what you see: “pile at the pin, thick at the boat, thin in between.” Watch the start before yours too. A fleet goes back to the same places race after race, and so do its piles.

The guess goes on your start card next to your first choice (chapter 9). If your first choice is where the pile will be, you know your second place before the crowd has formed.

On the waterAt three minutes before your next start, look along the line and say where the fleet is thick and where it is thin, in a few words: “pile at the pin, thin in the middle.” You’ve got it when where the piles are at the gun matches what you said at three minutes, in most of your starts.

10.4Stay out of the piles

A pile is a wall of boats within a few lengths of one end, all wanting the same place. One of them wins the end. The rest start in the second row, slow and low, with no gap, and some of them are over early. In a big fleet a pile is also where a comes from, and chapter 12 shows what the starting flags do to a boat that was over.

Whether to join one is a question of risk, the last line of your start card. A pile is a bet: one chance in ten of the perfect start, nine of a poor one. Take the bet only when you need a big result and the end is clearly worth many boat lengths. On a normal day you do not need it.

The rule for a normal day is to count. At two minutes, count the boats within five lengths of the end you want. Eight or more, and you move to the thin place you found in section 10.3, or to the middle with your transit. If the pile is at the pin, that place is back to your right, so you get onto port, by a tack or a gybe, and sail back along behind the row, keeping clear of every boat on starboard S1. If the pile is at the committee boat, sail on along behind the row on starboard. Five lengths from the pile, in the front row and at full speed, you are within a length of the pile's winner after a minute and ahead of everyone else in it.

Moving early is the whole trick. At two minutes the thin place is still thin. At one minute the boats that gave up on the pile are moving there too.

On the waterAt two minutes before the start, count the boats within five lengths of the end you want. If there are eight or more, move to your second place. You’ve got it when you start in the front row more often than in a pile, and after each start you can say which pile you stayed out of.

10.5Choose your lane early

After the gun every boat sails on starboard, side by side, and what you need is a : a strip of clear wind, with room below you to and room above you to point. The boats on either side decide whether you have one. A boat close to and ahead gives you her dirty air. A boat close to leeward and ahead bends the wind off her sail into yours, so you point lower and slow down.

So choose your neighbours, not just your place. At three minutes, go to the part of the line you chose and look at the boats around it. You want a space between two boats: one to leeward that is level with you or a little behind, and no faster than you, and one to windward that leaves you room. Say their sail numbers out loud. That space is your lane.

Choose it at three minutes, because the lane you want at one minute belongs to someone else by then. The sailors who choose early take the neighbours they want. The sailors who arrive late take what is left, which is a pile or the second row.

Holding the lane to the gun, making the gap and keeping it, is chapter 11. For now the job is to pick it, early, and to sit in it.

On the waterAt three minutes before the start, pick the space between two boats where you will start, and say both sail numbers out loud. You’ve got it when at one minute you are still between the two boats you named at three, in most of your starts.

10.6Your first move after the gun

Your start card has a line for it: after the gun, hold starboard, tack early, or wait for the first shift. Decide before the five-minute signal, because in the first ten seconds after the gun there is no time to decide anything.

Hold starboard when your side is the left, or when your lane is good and the wind is steady. Sail fast and flat for a minute, with your eyes on the sail, the water ahead and the boats close to you, not on the far-away fleet. If the lane is dying, so that you need the tiller all the time just to keep clear air, leave it early (chapter 13).

Tack early when your side is the right. From the committee boat end you can go after a few lengths with nobody to cross (chapter 9). From the middle you cannot: every boat to your right is on starboard, and a boat on port keeps clear of all of them S1. Each duck costs a length. So from the middle, hold until the fleet spreads out or a hole opens behind the boat to windward of you, usually a minute or two in.

Wait for the first shift when the day is swinging (chapter 4): hold starboard in your lane, watch the bow against the far shore, and tack on the first real header if your lane lets you. Say your move before the start, and after the gun do it. A move chosen in the rush of the gun is usually late.

On the waterBefore the five-minute signal, say your first move out loud: “hold”, “tack early” or “wait for the shift”. After the gun, do it for a minute, unless the thing on your card's “change the plan if” line happens. You’ve got it when a minute after the gun you are doing what you said before the start, and when you are not, you can say which line of the card made you change.
