Part V is about the boat. Everything in the first four parts, the wind, the plan, the start and the beat, pays only if the boat does what you ask of it. This chapter is the platform: why strategy needs a fast boat, the check before you launch, how the settings change from light wind to strong, and how the telltales tell you which gear you are in.

19.1Strategy needs a fast boat

A good choice of side can be worth seven lengths. A slow boat gives that back over one beat, because a boat that is two per cent slower than the boats around her loses nearly a length a minute, all the way up the beat. Strategy makes an advantage; speed is what turns it into distance.

The coaching standard for an Optimist sailor climbs in steps, and each step is a thing you can practise. Keep the sail filled and stop over-steering. Keep the heel constant, the boat flat and quiet. Work the mainsheet all the time, not only when something happens. Answer every gust and lull on purpose. And know your gears: speed, VMG, point and pinch (section 19.4), and change between them when you mean to.

The platform under all of that is a boat that is reliable and settings that are yours. A number copied from a faster sailor is a place to start, not an answer: your weight, your sail and the water you sail on change it. The fast setting is the one you found beside a training partner, one change at a time, and wrote down.

So the rule for Part V is simple. Before you think about the course, make sure the boat can do what the course will ask: accelerate out of a tack, stay flat in a gust, point when a lane needs it. Then the rest of the book works.

On the waterIn your next training session, pick one step of the ladder, a flat boat or a working mainsheet, and sail one whole beat thinking only about that step while your coach watches. You’ve got it when your coach can name the step you were working on without being told, and your speed beside a partner is the same or better than it was the week before.

19.2Check before you launch

A boat that fails costs more than any shift, and you matter more than the boat, so the check before you launch is part of racing. Eight things, every time, in the same order. First you: your buoyancy aid on and fastened, with its whistle tied to it. Then what keeps the boat afloat: the air bags firm and tied in, the painter and the paddle tied on.

Hull and bailers: the boat empty and dry, and the bailers tied in and working where you can reach them. Water in the boat is weight that slides to the low side, and it makes the boat heavier to accelerate and harder to keep flat.

Rudder and daggerboard: the rudder secured so it cannot lift out, the board tied to the hull, held in its case and free to move; raising it a little is also a way to depower in strong wind, so it must work. Sail ties: even gaps, every tie secure, the corners tied the way your sail's guide shows, so that the sail has the shape it was cut for.

Mast and : the mast secured, your mast rake (how far the mast leans) noted at the setting you start from, the sprit moving when you ask. and : running free, with the marks you set visible so that a change between wind ranges takes seconds. Hiking straps: set so that you can hike hard and still work the sheet and the tiller.

Say the eight out loud as you go: “aid, whistle; bags, painter, paddle; dry, bailers; rudder, board; ties; mast, sprit; vang, outhaul; straps”. Two minutes on the shore buy a boat you can forget about on the water, which is the point.

On the waterBefore your next launch, say the eight checks out loud in order as you do them, and touch each thing as you name it. You’ve got it when you do the eight checks without a list, in under two minutes, and nothing on your boat has failed in your last five races.

19.3Settings for light, medium and strong wind

The sail wants a different shape in each kind of wind, and your sailmaker's guide gives the numbers for your sail. This section is the shape of the answer, not the numbers.

Light wind: keep enough depth in the sail to accelerate, and do not over-sheet; the boom is usually a little further out. Move smoothly, so that you do not shake the wind out of the sail. Upwind, speed before height: both luff streaming; and a little leeward heel can help, lifting some hull out of the water and holding the sail's shape, so try it gently and see whether it is faster.

Medium wind: a powered sail and a firm , the boom end about over the back corner of the boat on the leeward side, then tuned with the leech telltale and the feel of the boat. Sail flat, use as little rudder as you can, and after a tack or a bad wave give up a little height for speed until the boat is going again.

Strong wind: a flatter sail with the top opened as your sail's guide says, a firm vang, a tighter outhaul, an active mainsheet, and only after testing it in practice, a little sprit ease or a little board raised, with a check that you can still tack. Hike early, keep the boat flat and dry, ease in the gust and trim again as control comes back.

Whatever the wind: change one thing at a time, test it beside a partner, and write down the wind, the waves, the setting and whether it was fast. That notebook is worth more than any number borrowed from a faster boat.

On the waterIn your next three sessions, test one setting change each session beside a training partner, and write down the wind, the waves, the change and the result before you go home. You’ve got it when you have a written page of settings for light, medium and strong wind in your own hand, and you can say which one you tested last and what happened.

19.4Telltales and your gears

Your sail has two kinds of telltale, and they answer two different questions. The pairs on the front of the sail, one each side, say whether the air is flowing over both sides of the sail: your angle to the wind, for the sheet you have set. If the sheet is wrong, fix that before you steer to them. The telltale on the top batten (the top one, if your sail has more) says what the air is doing as it leaves the back of the sail: your leech tension and your power.

Streaming off the leech means power and acceleration; stalling now and then means a higher-pointing trim. One coach's baseline is flowing about half the time and hiding behind the sail the other half when the trim is right, adjusted for what you need: more flow for speed, more stall for height. A leech telltale that never comes out is usually not pointing but over-sheeting: ease a little, and check your speed.

The front telltales give you your . Speed mode: both streaming, a little lower, for acceleration, waves, the first seconds after a tack, escaping bad air. VMG mode (VMG is short for velocity made good, the part of your speed that actually takes you upwind, straight towards where the wind comes from): the leeward one streaming and the windward one just starting to lift, your normal fast angle upwind. Point mode: more lift on the windward one, the leeward one still attached, for protecting a lane or holding height when your speed is secure. Pinch: higher still, the front of the sail going soft, only for a few seconds and never as a long slow habit (chapter 13).

The gears are a controlled change, not four fixed angles. You pick one for a reason, say it, and change back when the reason has gone: “speed mode out of the tack, now VMG”. A sailor who knows which gear she is in can choose; a sailor who does not is in whichever gear the last wave left her.

On the waterIn your next beat, say your gear out loud every time you change it, with the reason: “speed, out of the tack”, “point, for the lane”, “VMG”. You’ve got it when your coach hears the gear changes from the coach boat, each with a reason, and you are back in VMG within a few seconds every time the reason has gone.
