Chapters 1 to 4 were about the wind. Two more things move your boat without touching the sail: the water itself, when it flows, and the waves on it. This chapter shows how current moves the course and the start line, how to test it, and how to sail through waves and choose the tack that meets them best. At your club there is almost no current, but the waves you know well.

5.1How current moves the course

is the water itself moving. Rivers make it, tides make it, and so does a wind that has blown across a bay for hours. Everything that floats moves with it. The marks do not: they are anchored, and the water flows past them.

Think of the water as a rug pulled slowly across the floor. Every boat on the rug is carried the same way, so a current that is the same everywhere changes nothing between you and the boats around you. It changes where you all go: the marks and the line are not on the rug.

So current makes your bow lie. It points one way, and you really travel a little sideways, towards wherever the water is going. The laylines move, the start line moves, and a mark you point at slides off your bow (section 5.3).

Current decides a side only when it differs across the course: stronger in deep water than near the shore, or on one side of a point. Then the side with less current against you, or more with you, is worth going to, and in strong current that beats a small shift.

In your bay there is almost no current: the tide is 10 to 20 centimetres, and only a day of hard wind gives the water a little push. Learn this chapter for regattas elsewhere, where a river, a tide or a strait can move the water at a knot or more, a third of your own speed.

On the waterBefore the start at a place you do not know, look at an anchored mark for ten seconds and say out loud whether the water is moving past it, and which way. You’ve got it when you say “no current” at home on a normal day, and at a new place your call agrees with the sponge test of section 5.2.

5.2Test the current

You cannot see water move on its own, only against something that does not move: an anchored mark, a moored boat, a rock.

Sail close to a mark and look at the water around it. Moving water leaves a small wake on the side it is flowing to, and piles up a little on the side it comes from. No wake means little or no current. Do not use a coach boat as your fixed point: it is not anchored.

For a proper test, drop a wet sponge or a nearly full bottle next to the mark and sail a slow circle round it for one minute. The way it drifts is the way the current runs, and how far it drifts from the mark tells you how strong: a boat length in a minute is almost nothing, ten boat lengths is a current to plan around. Pick it up afterwards. A nearly full bottle is better than a sponge when there is wind: it floats low, so the wind hardly pushes it.

Test in two places: near the shore and in deep water, or at both ends of the course. Current is usually weaker in shallow water, so the difference between the two is what you are looking for. Look at the anchored committee boat too: the water leaves the same wake past its hull.

Do it before the first start at every regatta away from home, and again before each race where there is tide: the water turns round during the day. Say what you found out loud: “no current”, or “current from the left, two lengths a minute”.

On the waterWell before the first start at a new place, drop a nearly full bottle or a wet sponge next to a mark, time one minute, and count how many boat lengths it drifted from the mark, and which way. Then pick it up. You’ve got it when you can say the direction and the number of boat lengths before the start, and the wake on the marks agrees with your direction.

5.3Current and the start line

The start line is anchored, and you are on the moving rug. In the last minute before the gun that matters more than anywhere else.

Current towards the course pushes you over the line. Boats sit behind the line, and the water carries them across before the gun. Hold back further than usual, and check your often, because a boat that is has to come back against the current, which can cost the whole race.

Current away from the course pushes you back from the line. Everyone is late, the boats in the middle back from it, and a boat that stops moving drifts backwards. Stay close to the line, keep the boat moving, and set up nearer the line than feels right.

Current across the line pushes the whole fleet towards one end. Do not get stuck at that end: there is no room there, and the boats get swept onto the mark or over the line. Set up a little up-current of where you want to be, and leave space.

On the beat, the same current moves the laylines. Picture your last tack into the mark. If the water comes from the side your sail is on, it carries you up to the mark: tack before the usual layline. If it comes from the other side, it pushes you below the mark: sail past the usual layline before you tack. Aim for where you will be, not where your bow points.

On the waterIn any current, say out loud before the start which way it pushes you: over the line, back from it, or towards one end. Then set up on the other side of that. You’ve got it when at the gun in a race with current you are on the line and moving: not over early, not drifting back.

5.4Waves: pick your tack and your moment

Waves slow an Optimist more than almost anything. Every wave the bow climbs takes speed away, and a hard hit on the windward side of the bow throws water in and stops you. The embat builds across your bay by the afternoon, and at regattas on open water the waves can be much bigger.

Through the waves, keep your speed first. Head up a little as a wave picks up the bow, bear away over the crest, ease the sheet a touch and let the boat speed up down the back of the wave, then sheet in again. Sit still over the middle of the boat and let it pitch under you. And do not pinch: a slow boat gets stopped by the next wave.

Waves do not always come from where the wind comes from. After a shift, or with swell from the open sea, one tack meets them head-on and slams, while the other crosses them at a slant and runs smoother. When the wind gives neither tack much, choose the smoother one.

Wind blowing against a current makes the waves steeper and closer together, so a day with current is often a day with bad waves too.

Pick your moment to tack. A tack in the steepest waves stops the boat dead. Wait for a flat patch between the sets, then tack, unless a header is so big that waiting costs more.

On the waterOn a wavy day, say before the start which tack is smoother, and tack only in the flat patches between the sets. You’ve got it when you cross the waves at a slant on the smoother tack whenever the wind lets you, and your tacks do not stop the boat.
