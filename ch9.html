This is the first chapter of Part III, the start. Part II gave you a plan and a side; now you take them to the line. This chapter shows why the start is the first move of the beat, how to find the favoured end, why that end is often not the best place to start, how to start where your side is, why a gap beats the perfect end, and the five lines of your start card.

9.1The start is the first move of the beat

Crossing the line first is not the aim. The aim is to round the first mark in the leading group, and the start is the first move towards it: the gun does not finish anything, it opens the beat.

So the question before every start is not only which end. It is: where on the line do I get a start, at full speed, in clear air, and on the way to the side I want? A small advantage at the favoured end is worth less than a front-row start in clear air, because costs more in the first minute than a boat length or two of line.

Five things decide the place. Which end is further upwind (section 9.2). Which side of the beat you want (section 9.4). Whether current pushes the fleet towards one end or over the line (chapter 5). Where the crowd will be (section 9.5). And whether you could still tack out of that place if it goes wrong.

Three questions, said out loud, cover most of it: Which end? Which side do I want? Where is my ? The rest of this chapter answers them one at a time, and section 9.6 puts the answers on a card.

On the waterBefore your next start, answer the three questions out loud, a word or two each, before the five-minute signal: which end, which side, where is my gap. You’ve got it when you can say your three answers before the five-minute signal, and at the gun you are starting where they said.

9.2Which end? The head-to-wind test

The start line is almost never exactly square to the wind. One end is a little further upwind than the other, and a boat starting there is ahead on the ladder from the first second. That end is the .

The test: in the middle of the line, before the start, until you are , the sail flapping down the middle of the boat. Now look along the line. Compared with square across it, your bow leans a little towards one end. That end is further upwind: it is the favoured end. If the bow leans towards neither, the line is square. Do it only where no boat is close behind or beside you, and look under the boom first: a boat stopped head to wind cannot get out of the way.

Do it three times: at about ten minutes, at six, and just after the five-minute signal. The wind shifts, and the favoured end shifts with it: a right shift moves it towards the , a left shift towards the . On a swinging day it jumps from one end to the other. On a one-way day it moves more and more to one end, and will be even more favoured at the gun.

Say the answer out loud each time: “pin”, “boat” or “square”. And say how much. A bow that leans only a little can still mean an end several boat lengths ahead on a long line, and most of that only for the boats near the very end. A bow that leans a lot means an end worth many, and that changes everything in section 9.3.

On the waterAt about ten minutes, at six and just after the five-minute signal, sit head to wind in the middle of the line and say which end your bow leans to: “pin”, “boat” or “square”, and how much. Do it in a spot with no boat close to you. You’ve got it when your coach agrees with your call at each check, and you say so when it changes from one check to the next.

9.3Favoured end or best place to start?

The favoured end is a fact about the line. The best place to start is a fact about you, the fleet and the beat. They are not the same thing, and it is the second one that wins races.

Everyone can do the head-to-wind test, so the favoured end is where the crowd goes. A crowd at one end means dirty air, no room to bear away, boats over early and the end mark in the way. The one boat that wins that end does well. The twenty boats packed around it start slow and low, and lose more than the end was worth.

So weigh it. An end worth one or two boat lengths is not worth a fight with forty boats. An end worth many boat lengths, on a day when the wind keeps turning towards it, is worth going for, but with a plan for the crowd (section 9.5) and a second place in mind (section 9.6).

The aim is three things at once: the front row, full speed at the gun, and a clear way to the side you want. A start that gives all three a quarter of the way along the line beats a start that wins the end and loses the first minute. Winning the end is not winning the start.

On the waterBefore each start, say in one sentence which end is favoured and, in a second sentence, where you will start and why. You’ve got it when you can give both sentences before every start, and over a day's racing you are in the front row with clear air in most starts.

9.4Start where your side is

Chapters 1, 4 and 5 give you a side: more wind, the way the wind is turning, less current against you, and chapter 8 made it a method. Now start at the end nearest your side.

After the gun every boat is on starboard, heading left. From the pin end you are already the furthest boat to the left, and nobody can stop you holding starboard. From the committee boat you are the furthest to the right and can tack onto port a few lengths after the gun with nobody to cross, if no boat is close to windward or just behind. From the wrong end, the whole fleet lies between you and your side.

The middle is the third choice: fewer boats, and both sides still open. Its cost: you cannot see the ends, and the middle of the fleet sags back from the line, so you need a (chapter 10).

When the side and the favoured end disagree, a strong side wins: more wind you can see, a one-way day, a real current. A weak side and a strong end: take the end. Both weak: the middle, in the front row.

At the committee boat there is a trap: a boat that squeezes in between it and a boat below in the last seconds has no right to room, because the mark rules do not apply at a starting mark and a windward boat keeps clear S2. Come to that end with room below you.

On the waterBefore the start, say which side you want and which end is nearest to it. Start in the quarter of the line nearest that end, or in the middle if you want neither side. You’ve got it when after the gun you reach your side without crossing the fleet.

9.5A gap beats the perfect end

Whatever end you choose, what you need at the gun is a : open water just below you, a boat length or two, to into and speed up in the last ten seconds. Without it you cross the line slow, and a slow boat at the gun is in dirty air ten seconds later.

A gap near the end you want beats the perfect end without one. Full speed at the gun is worth more than a boat length of line, and the gap keeps your choices open: with clear air and room to tack you can still go either way after a minute. Room above you matters too when you want to tack soon: a boat close to pins you on starboard.

The crowd piles up at the favoured end and at the committee boat. Stay out of the piles: start a few lengths from the end, or in the middle, where a gap is easier to find and to keep. When an end is clearly worth many lengths, the pile is still a bet that only a bold start takes (section 9.6): a normal start goes beside it, a few lengths along, in the front row.

Making a gap and keeping it to the gun is chapter 11. For now the choice is this: end or gap? The gap, unless you need a bold start.

On the waterAt one minute before the gun, say where your gap is. If there is none, move along the line until there is one, even if that means a less favoured place (chapter 11 shows how). You’ve got it when you cross the line at full speed with clear air in most starts, and you can tack when you want to in the first minute.

9.6Your start card

Before the start, put your plan on a card: five lines, said out loud or written on the deck. Where I start: near the pin, the middle, or near the committee boat. If it is full: my second place. After the gun: hold starboard, tack early, or wait for the first shift. Change the plan if: one clear thing, such as no gap at one minute. How much risk: careful, normal or bold.

Careful means a steady start in the front row somewhere, when that is enough. Bold means fighting for the end, only when the end is clearly worth it, the side is sure, and you need a big result. Most starts are normal.

The card does two things. It makes you decide while you can still think, before the last minute, when there is no time to decide anything. And it tells you when to give the plan up: the “change the plan if” line is the one sailors skip, and the one that saves a race.

After the race, read the card again. Did you start where it said? If not, why? One honest reason, one thing for next time. Chapter 21 gives you a race card that keeps these answers race after race.

On the waterSay your five lines out loud before the five-minute signal: where, second place, after the gun, change if, how much risk. You’ve got it when you can say all five without stopping to think, and after the race you can say which line held and which did not.
