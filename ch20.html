A gust is a patch of stronger wind; a lull is a patch of lighter wind. Chapter 1 taught you to see them coming. This chapter is what you do when they arrive: how to be ready before the boat heels, the five stages of learning the gust, what to do when the wind drops, and how to keep control when it is really windy. The aim in every case is the same: the boat at the heel you want, a straight rudder, and speed kept through the change.

20.1See the gust before it hits

A is never a surprise if you are looking. It shows as darker, rougher water coming down the course, and as the boats ahead of you heeling and hiking before you feel anything. Chapter 1 gave you the look upwind every twenty seconds; this is what the look is for.

The best reaction to a gust begins before the boat heels. By the time the boat is over on its side, the rudder is already a brake and the sail is already pushing you sideways. So the moment you see the gust coming, get ready: body ready at the edge, hand on the sheet, eyes still up. When it arrives your body goes out and the sheet goes out together, and you are already doing the right thing.

Say it: “gust, ten seconds” when you see the dark water (ten is a guess; say it anyway), “now” when it reaches the boats ahead, and you will find that your body moves on “now” without being told.

The same look tells you more. A gust blows down the course with the wind: one straight upwind of you will reach you, one off to the side passes you by (section 1.1). And a gust may bring its own direction with it (section 4.6). The boats already in it show you both: how hard they heel (section 1.4), and whether the bows of the boats on your tack have swung up or down compared with yours.

On the waterIn your next beat, say “gust, ten seconds” out loud every time you see dark water coming, and “now” when it hits, and be ready to hike before “now”. You’ve got it when your coach sees you hike and ease before the boat heels in most gusts, and you can say after the race how many gusts caught you unprepared.

20.2Learn the gust in five stages

The coaching manual teaches the gust in five stages, and you learn them in order, one at a time; in the gust itself your body and your sheet move together, as section 20.1 says. Each stage only works once the one before it is a habit.

One, flat boat: ease the sheet enough to keep the boat from heeling and keep the course nearly straight. Two, hike before the heel: your body goes out early, not after the boat is already overpowered. Three, a tiny : once the boat is fast and flat, head up a little, and only a little; a big turn or a lot of rudder slows you down. Four, the exit: as the gust fades, trim the sheet back in smoothly, and hold your course for a moment before you come back down to your normal angle. Five, the advanced stage: use the speed the gust gave you for a controlled higher , then come back to your normal angle.

Some coaches say “hike, ease, trim” and some say “ease first, keep it flat”. The aim is the same in both: see it coming, stop the heel, keep the rudder quiet, and get the trim back smoothly, so that you keep useful speed through the whole gust.

The sentence for the water is the stage you are on: “flat boat” for the first weeks, then “hike before heel”, then “tiny feather”, then “wait, then trim”. When all four are habits, the fifth, “speed to height”, is yours.

On the waterWith your coach, pick the stage you are on and say it out loud in every gust of your next session: “flat boat”, “hike before heel”, “tiny feather” or “wait, then trim”. You’ve got it when your coach has seen you do your stage in every gust of a session without being reminded, and has moved you on to the next one.

20.3Lulls

A is the gust's opposite, and the answer starts from the other side of the boat: weight in first, then a small ease if the sail needs it, and the course last.

Move your weight in at once, so that the boat keeps the same heel instead of rolling over towards you, to windward; ease the sheet a little if the sail needs it to keep the air flowing; and let the boat keep moving. Do not make a big turn away from the wind out of habit. A boat that is still moving coasts through a short lull on its own speed.

If the lull lasts, then sail a little lower to keep the sail working, and only as much lower as it takes. And use the quiet: a lull is the best moment of the beat to look up the course for the next patch of pressure, instead of staring at the sail. Chapter 1 and chapter 4 tell you what to look for.

Say it: “lull: in, ease, coast”, and then “look”. Four words, and the boat keeps its speed and its height while the boats around you turn away and sail low.

On the waterIn your next session, every time the wind drops say “in, ease, coast, look” out loud and do each one in that order, and bear away only if the lull is still there after the look. You’ve got it when your coach has not seen you make a big bear-away in a short lull, and you can say what you saw up the course during the lulls of a beat.

20.4When it is really windy

In strong wind the order of jobs is fixed, and the first job is control. Hike as hard as you can while you still hold the sheet and the tiller, keep the boat dry, work the mainsheet all the time and steer with small corrections; a tiller pulled hard against a heeling boat is a brake, not a cure.

Then flatten the sail, using the settings your sailmaker gives for your sail (chapter 19). If you are still overpowered, test the bigger changes in practice first, never in a race, and one at a time: a little less tension, a little daggerboard raised. After any change, check that you can still tack and still get out of .

A brief flap of the sail is better than a hard heel. When the boat has too much power, the sheet gives first: ease until the front of the sail flaps for a moment, keep the boat flat and dry, and trim again as control comes back. A boat that heels hard and fills with water is slower in every gust that follows.

Higher pointing is the last thing you add, and only when you have speed and control to spare. The order is control, flat, dry, then fast, and only then high: flat means a flat boat, which the sheet giving first and the flatter sail are for; dry means no water in the boat, because a wet boat is slower in every gust after. In a big fleet on a windy day the sailors who kept that order are the ones in control at the top mark.

Strong wind means capsizes, and a capsize is normal. Stay with the boat and hold on to it: it floats, and you do not need to swim anywhere. Right it if you can. If you cannot, or you are tired or cold, raise one arm, blow your whistle and wait for the safety boat. And you never have to keep going: nobody can make you race S7. It works one way only: if your coach or a safety boat tells you to stop, you stop. You can come in at any time. Tell your coach, a safety boat or the committee boat, and go in once one has seen you. If nobody sees you and you are getting cold, sail in and tell an adult the moment you land. Coming in is a good decision, and there will be other races. Ask your coach to practise capsizes with you before you need one.

On the waterOn your next windy day, say the order out loud before the start, “control, flat, dry, fast, high”, and in every gust ease until the front of the sail flaps for a moment rather than let the boat heel. You’ve got it when your boat has little water in it at the windward mark, your coach has seen the sail flap briefly in gusts instead of the boat heeling hard, and you can tack without stalling in the strongest gusts of the day.
