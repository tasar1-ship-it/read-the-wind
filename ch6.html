Part I taught you to read the wind and the water. Part II turns the reading into a plan. This chapter is the first of three, and it is about what a good decision is: what you are really trying to do on the first beat, the difference between the plan and the moves, the three things every good choice keeps, the five things you read to make it, which comes first when they disagree, and the loop of looking and choosing that runs all the way from the warning signal to the mark. Chapters 7 and 8 then show how to read the race area and how to choose your side.

6.1Reach the first mark in the leading group

Ask most sailors what a good start is and they say winning the line. The coaching manuals say something else. The real objective of the start and the first beat is to reach the first mark in the . Winning the line is one way to get there. It is not the only way, and it is not the objective.

The best place to start is the place that reaches the first mark fastest, once you have counted everything: the bias of the line, the pressure, the shifts, the current, the crowd, and the tack you want to be on first. A small line advantage is often worth less than a start in clear air, and chapter 9 shows why the favoured end and the best place to start are so often different places.

This changes what you look at before the start. The line is studied last, after the race area, because the start is the first move of the beat and not a race of its own: a brilliant start that leads nowhere is a poor start. And it changes what you measure afterwards: not where you were at the gun, but where you were a minute later, and where you rounded.

In a fleet of seventy boats the leading group at the first mark is the front bunch; call it the first ten or fifteen, a rough number of this book's and not a rule. You can round in it after a middling start, and you can win the pin and miss it; the first of the two is the better sign of a sailor who can race a beat.

On the waterBefore your next start, say out loud what the objective is, the first mark in the leading group, then say which side of the beat you want and where on the line gives you a front row on the way to it, and only then check which end is favoured and weigh the two. You’ve got it when you can say after a race where you rounded the first mark and why, and you judge your start by that and not by the gun.

6.2Strategy and tactics

Two words that sound alike and mean different things. is the plan: which side of the beat, which tack first, how far from the middle you are willing to go. It comes from the conditions, the wind, the water and the course, and it would be the same plan if you were the only boat on the water. are what you do about the boats near you while you carry out the plan: cross, tack or , the , holding a or leaving it, a rival.

The first-beat manual puts it in one line: the conditions choose the plan; the boats nearby affect how you carry it out. The plan is made before the start from what Part I taught you to read, and written on your card (chapter 21). The tactics are decided at each meeting, in seconds, with the plan in your head.

The order matters. Tactics serve the strategy, never the other way round. A port-tack boat that ducks a starboard boat to keep going to the side it wants has made a tactical loss of a length and kept its plan. A boat that tacks on a starboard boat's lee bow to win the meeting and sails away from its side has won the meeting and lost the plan. Chapter 16 calls this the smallest loss that keeps the plan.

So when a move near another boat tempts you, ask what it does to the plan. If it sends you the wrong way, the meeting is not worth winning. The exception is a loss so large that the plan has already gone, a forced tack onto a or a stop in ; then the job is a new plan.

On the waterIn your next race, say “plan” or “boats” out loud each time you decide something on the beat, so that you know which kind of decision you are making. You’ve got it when you can say after a race which of your decisions were strategy and which were tactics, and the tactical ones kept you going the way the plan said.

6.3Fast, clear, correct

The coaching manual's central rule is three words. Be fast, be in clear air, and be on the correct side. Fast means boat speed: a flat boat, a sail that is drawing, the of chapter 19. Clear means undisturbed wind and room to move: a lane of your own (chapter 13) and a way out of it. Correct means the right side, the right tack and the right amount of risk: what chapters 4, 8, 14 and 15 are about.

The best decision is enough of all three, not one of them at the expense of the others. A move that looks clever but destroys your speed or traps you in dirty air is usually not clever. A tack onto a layline to cover one boat gives up the correct side for one rival; a to cross a bow that leaves you stopped gives up speed to keep your tack; a dive into a crowded corner gives up clear air for a side. Each pays with the three to buy something smaller.

The three are also the order to check when something has gone wrong. First, am I fast? If not, nothing else matters until the boat is moving: ease, flatten, bear away a touch, get the flow back. Then, is my air clear? If not, find a lane before the loss grows. Only then, am I on the correct side and the correct tack? A boat that answers the third question before the first two is sailing a plan it cannot carry out.

Say the three words when you are not sure what to do. If one is missing, that is the decision: get it back. If all three are there, the decision is to keep going, which is a decision too, and the easiest one to forget.

On the waterOn your next beat, every time you think about a move, say “fast, clear, correct” and name the one of the three the move would cost you, before you make it. You’ve got it when you have let a move go because it would have cost speed or clear air, and said so, at least twice in a race.

6.4Five things to read

Every decision on the first beat is made from five things, and you have met four of them already. The wind's direction: where it is now, which way it is turning, and what kind of day it is (chapters 2 and 4). The : where there is more wind, and whether it is coming to you or you must go to it (chapter 1). The : whether the water moves, which way, and how much (chapter 5).

The fourth is the course itself: the line and its bias, where the mark is, whether it sits straight upwind or off to one side, and so which tack is the . Chapter 7 shows how to find the mark and the long tack before the start, chapter 9 the line, and chapter 14 how to use the long tack when the picture is unclear.

The fifth is the fleet: where the boats are, where they will crowd, where the good sailors are going, and which boats are near enough to matter to you. It is the only one of the five that moves when you move, and the only one that can take your wind. Chapters 7, 13 and 15 are about reading it.

Five things are a lot to hold at once, and you are not asked to. Before the start you read all five slowly and write the answers on the card (chapter 21); during the beat three questions stay live (section 6.6) and the five answers sit behind them. What you must never do is decide from one of the five while forgetting the other four: a dark patch (pressure) on the wrong side of a big one-way shift (direction) has to be weighed, not chased (chapter 4), and a favoured end (course) with forty boats on it (fleet) is a trap.

On the waterBefore your next start, say the five answers out loud, one or two words each: direction, pressure, current, course, fleet. If one answer is “don't know”, that is the thing to look at next. You’ve got it when you can give all five answers before the five-minute signal without being asked, and you can say which of the five decided your side.

6.5What comes first

When several choices compete, the manuals give an order, so that you do not weigh small things while the big one gets away. First, is the move safe, under control and allowed by the rules? If not, reject it; there is nothing to weigh. Second, does it keep your speed? Protect speed before pointing and before any clever move. Third, does it keep your clear air and your room to move? A dying lane is changed early, before the loss grows. Fourth, where is the advantage on the course: the pressure, the lift, the favourable current? Lead towards it without sailing into a corner. Fifth, how much risk do your sureness and the race situation allow? Take more when behind; keep what you have when ahead (chapter 15 calls it banking).

The first-beat manual has a shorter list of its own for the beat: pressure first, then the lifted or the long tack, then clear air, then control of the fleet, each adjusted for how strong the signal is. Pressure comes first because in an Optimist more wind usually beats a small loss of angle (chapter 4). The lifted tack comes next on a swinging day, the tack towards the shift on a one-way day (chapter 4), and the long tack when the picture is unclear.

The two lists seem to disagree about clear air, and the strength of the signal is what settles it. When the pressure is big and sure, go to it with the fewest tacks and ducks you can manage, and keep your speed and your air on the way: the manual's words are the least disturbance, which means the fewest moves, not a dirty lane. A weak or unsure clue is not worth leaving a clean lane for; move towards it only while you can still change your mind. When nothing is strong, clear air and the middle come first, because they keep both sides open.

On the waterAt your next hard choice on a beat, say the five steps out loud in order, safe, fast, clear, where, how much risk, and stop at the first one the move fails. You’ve got it when you can say after a race which step decided each of your big choices, and none of them failed the first three.

6.6Look, choose, sail, look again

A decision is not a thing you make once before the start. It is a loop that runs from the warning signal to the mark: look, name what you see, choose, sail the choice, and look again. The first-beat manual calls it the sailor's loop; the illustrated guide shortens it to four words for the deck: look, decide, sail, look again.

Looking is the step that is easiest to skip, which is why chapter 1 made it a count of 20. Naming what you see turns a look into a decision: “lift”, “dark water left”, “boat on my wind”, said out loud. Choosing is quick when the first two were done. Sailing the choice means holding it long enough to find out, a few lengths at least. And looking again is what keeps the plan alive, because the wind that made it does not stay.

While the loop runs, three questions stay live, and only three. Is the boat fast? What will the wind do next? Which boats matter? The coaching manual gives a sailor one cue for speed, one for the wind and one for the fleet, and adds more only when those three have become automatic. Part I feeds the second question; chapters 13 to 16 the third; chapter 19 the first.

The loop has a bigger version too. Every big change, a settled shift, a band of pressure that has moved, a dying breeze, is a new look at the whole plan (chapter 14). And after the race the loop runs once more, slowly, with the five questions of chapter 23: what did you see, what did you plan, did the start give you access to it, what made you keep or change it, and would you decide the same again.

On the waterOn your next beat, say the loop out loud at least five times: “look” as you look up the course, what you see in two words, “choose” with the choice, and then sail it for at least a count of 20 before you change it. You’ve got it when the loop runs on its own, you can say what you named at each look of a beat, and your coach has heard you say “look again” before a change rather than after it.
