The last chapter had you read the four-minute flag. This one says what each flag means and what it does to the last minute: P, the normal start; I, the flag that sends you round an end; Z, a scoring penalty; U and black, which put you out of the race; and the rules at the two ends of the line, where rights are weakest and mistakes cost most. The flags are rules, and the rule book decides; the sources page says where each one is written.

12.1P: the normal start

At four minutes the race committee puts up a second flag beside the class flag, and that flag tells you how dangerous the last minute is. There are five: P, I, Z, U and black, and sometimes Z and I together. Learn the shapes and colours, not only the names, because you have to read them from a hundred metres away.

P is blue with a white square. It is the normal start, with no special penalty in the last minute. If you are over the line at the gun, the committee shows flag X with one sound, the , and you go back and start again: sail back until your whole hull is behind the line, then cross it properly S4. While you go back, keep clear of the boats that are starting S4.

Flag X does not say who was over. Everyone near the line looks at everyone else. If you think it might be you, the honest answer is usually yes: a boat that was clearly behind the line knows it. Going back costs a minute. Not going back when you were over costs the race, because a boat that was over and does not go back is scored as if it had not started S4.

Under P the line is cheap to test. Sailors push it, the fleet creeps, and a follows: one pennant, the First Substitute, blue with a yellow triangle inside it, with two sounds, and everyone starts again S4. That is when the committee changes the flag. Chapter 10 said where recalls come from: the piles. Under P, be in the front row on your transit and let the pile fight it out.

On the waterAt four minutes before your next start, say the flag out loud as it goes up: “P, normal.” If flag X goes up after the gun, say out loud whether you were over, and if you were, go back. You’ve got it when you can name the four-minute flag within a few seconds of it going up, and your coach has never seen you sail on after being over.

12.2I: round an end

I is yellow with a black circle. It comes out when the committee is tired of recalls. Under I, in the last minute before the gun, if any part of your hull gets over the line or over an , the line continued beyond the pin or beyond the committee boat, you have to come back round an end: sail round the pin or round the committee boat, across the extension, to the pre-start side before you can start S4. Dipping back through the middle of the line does not count.

That changes the last minute for the whole fleet. Under P a boat that is a little over at thirty seconds drops back a length and is fine. Under I the same boat has to sail to an end, round it and come back, which from the middle of a long line takes two or three minutes. So under I nobody wants to be near the line early, and the whole row sags back further than usual.

For you that means two things. Your transit is worth more, because the sag is bigger and you can be the one on the real line. And the margin: hold a length further back at thirty seconds than you would under P, and come to the line in the last ten seconds with the gap, not before.

The last minute begins when the four-minute flag comes down, with a sound, at one minute (chapter 11). Before that moment the I flag has not started working, and being over is no worse than under P: get back behind the line S4.

On the waterUnder the I flag, at thirty seconds before the gun, be one length further back than usual. Say your transit out loud and come to the line only in the last ten seconds. You’ve got it when under I you have never had to sail round an end, and at the gun you are on the line with the sagging row behind you.

12.3Z: a scoring penalty

Z is a flag of four triangles: red, yellow, blue and black. It uses a different shape on the water: not the line but , the water between the pin, the committee boat and the first mark. Under Z, in the last minute, if any part of your hull is inside that triangle and the committee identifies you, you get a scoring penalty of twenty per cent of the score for not finishing, added to your score, and going back does not erase it S4. Your score in a race is your place: tenth scores ten points, and not finishing scores one more than the number of boats S4.

In a regatta of seventy boats that costs about fourteen places. A good race under Z with a penalty is a bad race. And the penalty can follow you: if the start is recalled and you were identified, the penalty stays for the restart, and another breach there adds another S4.

The triangle is a different shape from the line. Under I, crossing an extension beyond the pin in the last minute is a breach; under Z it is not, because the extension is outside the triangle. Going back does not cancel the penalty: once you are identified, it is on the score sheet whether you start well or badly. But if you were over at the gun you must still go back and start S4. Flag X goes up as usual, and a boat that does not go back is scored as if it had not started: seventy-one points in a fleet of seventy, when the penalty on its own is about fourteen. Going back costs places, but it is never worse.

The rule for Z: give the line a margin, and never test it. One length further back at thirty seconds, the go at ten seconds and not at nine, and your transit checked twice in the last minute.

On the waterUnder the Z flag, say the three corners of the triangle out loud at four minutes: “pin, boat, mark.” At thirty seconds, be one length further back than usual. You’ve got it when under Z you have never had a scoring penalty, and you can draw the triangle on the beach after the race.

12.4U and black: out of the race

U is red and white in four squares. Black is plain black. Both use the triangle, like Z, and both end your race: under U or black, in the last minute, if any part of your hull is inside the triangle and the committee identifies you, you are disqualified from that race without a hearing S4. There is no twenty per cent and no going back, and no flag will tell you that you were identified: the rules do not use flag X under U or black S4, so do not wait for one, and going back changes nothing. The committee writes your number down, and you learn it ashore; under black you also see it on the committee boat after a general recall S4.

The difference is the restart. Under U, if the race is recalled or started again, the disqualification does not carry over: you start again with everyone else. Under black it does: after a general recall under black, the committee displays the sail numbers of the boats it identified, and a boat on that list is out of the restart as well S4. Look for the numbers. If yours is there, do not start.

Under black the fleet changes. The piles thin out, because nobody wants to be identified at the end of a crowded line. The sag grows. The boats that were going to win the pin at any price now hold a length further back. That is a day for your transit and for the margin.

The one thing never to do under U or black is the hopeful push: “I was probably behind.” Probably is not enough when the cost is the race. Give up a length. A boat one length behind the line at the gun, at full speed, is a front-row start; a boat on the list is nothing.

On the waterUnder U or black, at thirty seconds before the gun be one length further back than usual, about four lengths from the line, and say so out loud: “a length further back.” You’ve got it when under U or black you have never been disqualified at the start, after every general recall under black you read the sail numbers before doing anything else, and you can say after each start where you were at thirty seconds.

12.5Rules around the starting marks

Two things decide what you can do at the ends of the line. At a mark of the course the rule book gives an inside boat room to round it (chapter 18). Not at a starting mark: while the boats are approaching it to start, until they have passed it, the mark rules do not apply at the pin or at the committee boat S2. There is no room at the committee boat. A boat that comes in to windward of you there and asks for room has no right to it: she is the boat and keeps clear S2.

So the committee boat end has a trap on both sides. If you are the boat above, you must keep clear of the boat below you, and she can head up to stop you getting between her and the committee boat S2. If you are the boat below, close the door by heading up in good time. The rules on changing course and on avoiding contact, which say how far you may go, are chapter 18.

At the pin the trap is the buoy itself: a boat squeezed at the pin end with no room below her has nowhere to go but into it, and the rule book says what touching a mark costs (chapter 18 will cover it). A boat that comes in to windward at the pin has the same answer as at the committee boat: the windward boat keeps clear S2.

At the pin, be the boat with the gap below, or be a few lengths along the line. The ends are where rights are weakest and consequences biggest.

On the waterIn your next three starts, never come in to windward of another boat at either end of the line in the last thirty seconds. If the only place left is above someone at an end, take the next place along instead. You’ve got it when you can say after each start whether you were the windward or the leeward boat at the end you started near, and you have not asked for room at a starting mark.
