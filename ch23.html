The last chapter is the one you use after every race: five questions on the way in, a scorecard with eight parts, a way of tracking how you race rather than where you finish, and the rule that holds the whole book together: judge the decision, not the result. It is short on purpose. A debrief that takes four minutes happens after every race; one that takes an hour happens once.

23.1Five questions after every race

The debrief is five questions, asked in this order, and only five. What did you see before the start? What was your one-sentence plan for the first beat? Did your start give you access to that plan? Which new cue made you keep the plan, or change it? Would you make the same decision again with what you knew at that moment?

The first question tests your looking, not your hindsight: say what you saw, not what you know now. The second checks that there was a plan at all, and that it fits in one sentence. The third joins the start to the beat, which is the book's whole point. The fourth finds the cue that decided the beat. The fifth separates the decision from the result.

Your coach asks the questions and then digs under your answers with two questions from the coaching manual: what told you that? and which option did you keep? Never “you should have gone left”. The answer you find yourself is the one you will have next week.

Four minutes is enough, on the way in, with the race card (chapter 21) in your hand: the card gives the first two answers already written down, the drawing on the back shows where the fourth happened, and the change-if line shows whether the start trigger fired. Then one behaviour for the next race, the one on the card's next-time line, said out loud, because a review that does not end in one thing to do has not ended.

On the waterAfter your next race, before the boat is on the trolley, answer the five questions out loud in order, with your race card in your hand, and finish with the one behaviour for next time. You’ve got it when you can give the five answers in four minutes after any race, your coach has not needed to supply one, and you can say the one behaviour for next time without being asked.

23.2Your scorecard

Once a week, or after a regatta, score the first beat on the : eight parts, each 0, 1 or 2. It takes five minutes and it shows what the five questions cannot: the pattern across many races.

The eight rows: the look before the start; the start plan; the start itself; lanes; shifts and pressure; risk; the layline and the mark; the rules. In each row 0 means nothing useful, 1 means partly, and 2 means done well. The card says what a 2 is. The look: a map of wind, current and line, with how sure you were. Shifts and pressure: repeated calls on evidence. The start: the front row with speed and a route to your side. Lanes: protecting or escaping early. The layline: a late starboard approach with speed and a gap. The rules: right, early and safe.

The drawings on the backs of your race cards (chapter 21) are the evidence for the lanes, risk and layline rows. The 0s are the habits this book is against: no useful test, no place or no first tack, the second row, stopped or over early, staying too long in bad air, reacting or guessing, a separation you did not plan, an early layline or a forced port entry, unclear rights or a near miss.

Sixteen is a perfect beat and nobody sails one. The use of the card is the row that stays at 0 or 1 week after week: that row is your next month of practice (chapter 22).

On the waterAfter your next regatta, score each of the eight rows 0, 1 or 2 for each first beat of the regatta, and circle the row with the lowest score. You’ve got it when you have scorecards for the last four regattas, and the row you circled became the behaviour you practised in the sessions between them.

23.3Track how you race, not only where you finish

Your finishing place is one number, and it mixes five things: your decisions, your speed, luck (mostly the wind's), the strength of the fleet, and what went wrong elsewhere in the race. A place that goes up may mean a weak fleet; a place that goes down may mean a strong one. The number cannot tell you which.

So track the things that are yours. Front-row starts out of the total. Minutes in clear air on one beat, timed by your coach. Shift calls that were right against calls that were wrong. Early laylines, counted as the races where you reached one with the mark still far away. Rules risks, counted as the close moments where you were not sure of your rights, or nearly hit a boat. And how well your “how sure am I” matched what happened: were you right when you said sure, and when you were not sure, did you say so?

Six counts, kept race after race in a notebook, one page for the month; the session counts from chapter 22 go on the same page. Each one is a behaviour from this book, each one you can practise in a session from chapter 22, and each one moves on its own, which the place never does.

Over a season the counts tell a story the results cannot: the front-row starts that went from one in five to four in five, the early laylines that stopped, the shift calls that became right more often than wrong. That story is the one worth telling your coach, and it is the one that predicts next season's places.

On the waterStart a page with the six counts and fill it in after every race this month: front-row starts, minutes in clear air, right and wrong shift calls, early laylines, rules risks, and whether your sureness matched what happened. You’ve got it when you can say your six counts for the month without looking them up, and you can say which one moved most.

23.4Judge the decision, not the result

The fifth question is the one the whole book rests on: would you make the same decision again with the information you had at that moment? A good decision can lose, and a bad one can win, because the wind does unlikely things and seventy boats do more of them.

So every big choice gets two answers, kept apart, as chapter 16 says: was the choice good, on what you could see, and did it work. Good and worked: keep it. Good and did not work: keep it, if you can say what you saw that made it good. Bad and did not work: change it. Bad and worked: change it anyway, because luck does not repeat and a habit built on it will cost a series.

This is also how to listen to other sailors. A sailor who went to the corner on a guess and won has a result, not a method, and the corner will lose at least as often as it wins. A sailor who read a swinging day, sailed the middle on the headers and finished tenth in a seventy-boat fleet has a method, and the method will win more often than the corner over a season.

That is the end of the book, and the beginning of the racing. Look up. See the shift, and know what kind of day it is. Start clean. Choose well at every meeting, judge the choice and not the place, and let the odds do the rest.

On the waterAfter your next race, for your two biggest decisions, say the two answers out loud, good choice or bad choice and worked or did not work, and say what you would do again with the same information. You’ve got it when you have said “good choice, did not work” about a race that went badly, as in chapter 16, and your coach agreed.
