The windward mark is where a beat is cashed in. All the shifts, lanes and meetings of the last chapters end in one place: a buoy with seventy boats trying to get round it. This chapter is the last few hundred metres: how to see both laylines all the way up the beat, why reaching one early costs, what the starboard queue does to you, how to come in from below it and choose your last tack, what the port approach really risks, what changes inside the zone, how to plan the exit before the mark, and how to judge the beat afterwards.

17.1See both laylines

From the mark, two run down the course: the starboard layline out to the right, and the port layline out to the left. Everything between them is water with choices in it; everything outside them is wasted distance.

The job is to know where they are all the way up the beat, not only at the end. From the middle of the course you cannot see them, but you can picture them: two lines from the mark, each at the angle you sail close-hauled. The test from chapter 4 tells you how near you are: when the mark sits square across your boat, or a touch behind it to allow for slip, you are on that layline; while it is still ahead of square, you are inside it. The other layline is on your bow: on port, when the mark comes onto your bow you are on the port layline. So inside both means the mark between your bow and square across.

The laylines move. A shift swings both of them around the mark: a right shift pulls the starboard layline in towards the middle and pushes the port one out, and a left shift does the opposite. Current across the course bends them, and chop makes you point lower, so the real layline is a little further out than the one you drew in flat water (chapter 5). So look again from closer range; a layline called from far away is a guess.

Say where you are out loud: “mark between the bow and square, inside both”, or “nearly square on starboard, port layline close”. Square on port means the starboard layline is close; square on starboard means the port one. Knowing which layline is nearer is the first half of planning the last two tacks (chapter 14).

On the waterIn your next beat, three times on the way up, say out loud where the mark sits relative to square across your boat and which layline is nearer. You’ve got it when you can say at any moment of a beat which layline is nearer and roughly how far it is, and your coach agrees from the coach boat.

17.2Why early laylines cost

Reaching a layline early feels safe: the mark is in sight, one tack to go. It is the most expensive place on the course, because every option is gone and every shift from now on is a loss.

On the layline a header means you no longer the mark: you have to tack twice more, in the traffic, to get up to it. A lift means you have : you ease the sheet and reach down to the mark, sailing distance that nobody else sails. In the middle of the course the same shift would have been a choice; here it is only a bill, and the earlier you arrive, the bigger the bill.

There is a second cost. The laylines are where the fleet gathers, so an early layline is also a long queue in dirty air (section 17.3). And there is a third: a boat beyond the layline cannot answer anything. A boat tacking on her wind, a lull on her side, a gust on the other: she sails on and takes it.

Chapter 14 gave you the four-fifths warning for the middle of the beat. Near the mark it becomes a rule: do not reach a layline while the mark is still far away, and from the left, tack several lengths short of the port layline so that one more correction stays possible. Save the layline for the last few lengths, where the shifts have no room left to hurt.

On the waterIn your next race, say “layline” out loud at the moment you reach one, and say how far the mark still is. Try to make that distance shorter from race to race. You’ve got it when you reach your final layline within ten lengths of the mark more often than not, and you can say after the race where you reached it and why.

17.3The starboard layline queue

Most of the fleet comes into the mark on starboard, because starboard has right of way S1 and a mark left to port is rounded straight off a starboard approach. The result is a train: a line of boats on the starboard layline, bow to stern, each one in the of the boat ahead.

The train is slow. Every boat in it points a little lower and goes a little slower than the clean boat at its head, and the ones that joined far out spend a minute or more in bad wind before they reach the mark. They arrive behind boats they were level with at the top of the course. A boat pinching to stay on the line in dirty air is the slowest boat on the water.

So treat the queue as a place to pass through, not to wait in. Join it late, close to the mark, with a gap to the boat ahead of you so that your wind is your own. A few lengths of clean sailing below the line are worth more than a place in a slow train.

And never pinch into the transom of the boat ahead to hold your place. If the gap closes, bear away a little, take the speed, and come back up with room. Speed into the mark is what gets you round it; a place in the line is not.

On the waterIn your next race, look at the starboard train from below as you approach and say out loud where the gap is that you will join, and whose transom you will not pinch towards. You’ve got it when you join the train with clear air behind a boat you can name, and your coach has not seen you pinching in a queue.

17.4Come in from below the layline

The approach that keeps your choices is from below the starboard layline: a few lengths inside it, on port, looking at the train from the side. From there you still decide where and when the last tack happens.

Sail up on port a few lengths below the line and watch the train. When you see the gap you want and the mark comes square across the boat, tack into it with speed. Call the layline from close range, with the chop and the current included, a little behind square, and more in chop or light wind, as chapter 3 said. If you must be wrong, be a length late rather than a length early: a little overstanding costs a few metres of reaching, a little short costs two more tacks in the traffic.

Two things can go wrong, and both have an answer. If you cannot fetch, correct early: two short tacks while there is still room cost a few lengths; pinching until you stop beside the mark costs more, and touching the mark costs a penalty S7 (chapter 18). If you have overstood, keep the speed, ease the sheet, and sail straight to the mark; do not add tacks to a mistake unless you need clear air.

The sentence is “below the line, pick the gap, tack with speed”. It gives you a planned arrival instead of a desperate one.

On the waterIn your next race, approach the windward mark on port a few lengths below the starboard layline, say “gap” out loud when you see it, and tack into it with speed. You’ve got it when your last tack into the mark is a planned one into a gap, you fetch the mark without pinching, and you can say after the race where you tacked and why.

17.5The port approach

Sometimes you arrive at the mark on port: the left side paid, or the last shift put you there. It is the riskiest moment of the beat, and you should know exactly why.

Every boat in the starboard train has right of way over you S1. And the mark gives you nothing: on a beat the mark rules do not apply between boats on opposite tacks S6, so being nearer the mark than a starboard boat, even inside the , does not earn you room. A port boat has no inside position at the windward mark.

So the choice is the one from chapter 16, with less room. Cross only where there is a clear margin, a real gap that stays open as you watch. Otherwise early, pass behind the starboard boat, and tack into the train behind her with a gap of your own. The duck and the tack behind her cost two lengths; a failed cross costs the race.

Do not try to tack inside the zone in front of a boat that is already fetching the mark. Section 17.7 says what the rules add to that tack; the short version is that it works only into a gap so big that nobody has to change course at all.

The plan is to avoid the port approach: from the left, tack short of the port layline and come across to the starboard side early (section 17.2). When it happens anyway, decide from ten lengths out, as in chapter 16, and say it: “clear gap, cross” or “no gap, duck and join”.

On the waterIn your next race, if you arrive at the mark on port, say “cross” or “duck and join” out loud ten lengths away, and choose “cross” only with a gap you can see staying open. You’ve got it when you have not made a late turn in front of a starboard boat at the mark, and after the race you can say what your port approach cost.

17.6Your last tack

The last tack onto starboard for the mark is chosen on three things: the traffic, the shift and the pressure. Look at them in that order, ten to twenty lengths out, while there is still room to choose.

Traffic first: where is the gap in the train, and which boat will be ahead of you when you tack? Pick a gap with clean air and a boat ahead you can keep clear of, not the first space you see. The shift second: on port below the starboard layline, a header tells you to tack sooner, because the layline has moved towards you; a lift tells you to wait, because it has moved away. The pressure third: if a dark patch lies on one side of the approach, tack so that your last leg runs through it.

Then make the tack the best of the beat: flat, fast, speed first on starboard (chapter 13), with a length of clear water ahead so that you are not pinching from the first second.

Say the plan out loud before you do it: “gap behind the blue boat, headed, tack now”. A last tack that was said is nearly always a good one; a last tack made in a hurry is the one that misses the mark.

On the waterIn your next race, say your last-tack plan out loud before you make it: which gap, what the shift is doing, and where the pressure is. You’ve got it when your coach hears the plan before your last tack, and you fetch the mark in clear air with speed.

17.7The zone and mark-room

The is the water within three boat lengths of the mark S6. Inside it a new rule begins: . At this mark it works between boats on the same tack; on a beat, boats on opposite tacks are left out (below).

On the same tack it works like this. If two boats are when the first of them reaches the zone, the outside boat gives the inside boat mark-room S6: room to sail to the mark and round it. If one boat is clear ahead when she reaches the zone, the boat behind gives her mark-room from then on S6. What counts is the moment the boat ahead reaches the zone: a boat that was behind then and gets her bow inside later is owed nothing, and the boat that was ahead keeps her room even if an overlap begins or ends inside S6.

On a beat, between boats on opposite tacks, there is no mark-room at all S6. A port boat inside the zone is still only a port boat, and the starboard boat still has right of way S1.

There is one more rule, for the tack inside the zone. A boat that tacks from port to starboard inside the zone, at a mark left to port, owes two things to a boat that is fetching the mark and has been on starboard ever since entering the zone. She must not make that boat sail above close-hauled to avoid her, and she must give it mark-room if it becomes overlapped inside her S6. In practice: tack in the zone only into a gap so big that nobody alters course.

Before every close moment near the mark, run the five questions of chapter 18: which tack, overlapped or clear, who has right of way, is it limited, what is my safe way out.

On the waterIn your next race, as you enter the zone, say out loud which boats you owe mark-room to and which boats owe it to you, and which boats on the other tack owe you nothing. You’ve got it when you can say at the zone who owes room to whom every time, and in your last five races no boat with right of way or room over you has had to change course to avoid you at a windward mark.

17.8Plan the exit

The rounding is not the end of the beat; it is the start of the next leg, and the next leg is decided before the mark, not at it. In the rounding your hands are full and nothing new gets decided.

So ten to twenty lengths out, once the approach is settled, look past the mark. Which way does the next leg go, and which side of it has the wind? Where will the boats ahead of you be, and where is the clear air after the mark? Where do you want to be thirty seconds after rounding? Decide that, and say it: “round close, bear away, go low to clear air”.

Then the rounding itself: come in with speed, round close to the mark without touching it, bear away with the boat flat and the sheet easing as you turn, and leave with the boat already set up for the new leg. A wide, slow rounding gives the boat behind you the inside of the next leg; a tight, fast one keeps it.

Do the next-leg jobs, the sail controls, the board, only when the approach is secure. A sailor fiddling with the sprit two lengths from the mark in a train is a sailor about to be rolled.

On the waterIn your next race, say your exit plan out loud before you enter the zone: which way after the mark and where the clear air is. Then round close and fast. You’ve got it when you can say your exit plan before the zone every time, and you leave the mark with clear air and the boat set up for the next leg.

17.9Judge the beat by your choices

After the rounding you know your place. The place is one number, and it is a poor judge of the beat, because it mixes what you chose with what the wind did.

Judge the beat by the choices instead, the way chapter 16 judges a meeting. Ask five questions. Did I see both laylines all the way up? Did I join the layline late, near the mark? Did I come in from below on starboard, into a gap, with clear air? Did I know my rights at the zone, and the ones I did not have? Did I plan the exit before the mark?

Five yeses and a bad place is a good beat with a bad shift in it, and you keep every choice for next time. Five noes and a good place is luck, and it teaches nothing unless you say so. Most beats are three or four yeses, and the missing one is next week's job.

Say it after every race, with your coach if you can: the five answers, then the place. In that order, because the choices are yours every race and the place is yours only on average. That is how a big fleet is learned: one honest beat at a time.

On the waterAfter your next race, before you say your place at the windward mark, say the five answers out loud: laylines seen, layline joined late, in from below with a gap, rights known, exit planned. You’ve got it when you can give the five answers after every race, and you have named the one to work on next week.
