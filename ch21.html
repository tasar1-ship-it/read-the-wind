21The race card

Part VI is about every race, and this chapter is the tool: one small race card, filled in before the start and after the race, with a drawing of the first beat on the back. It is where the look, the head-to-wind test, the plan, the first move and the honest reason meet, race after race. This chapter shows the card line by line, and ends with the pocket cards: the few words you say at each moment of a race.

21.1Before the start: tap what you see

The first half of the is filled in before the start, after the look (chapter 1) and the head-to-wind test (chapter 9), while you can still think. You tap what you see, not what you hope.

Five lines, each a choice between three answers on this half of the card. Shift today: swinging, one-way (and say which way, as section 4.4 does), or not sure (chapter 4). More wind on: the left, the right, or the same (chapter 1). Favoured end: the pin, the boat, or square (chapter 9). Where I start: near the pin, the middle, or near the boat. And then the first move, which section 21.2 is about.

Every answer is something you can see or test. If you cannot fill a line in from evidence, the answer is not sure, and not sure means the middle: the middle of the line with a transit (chapter 10), the middle of the course on the long tack (chapter 4), both sides open. Check the favoured-end line again at the last head-to-wind test (chapter 11).

Under them sit four lines from your start card (chapter 9), nine lines in all, that make the plan work in a crowd: if it is full, my second place; after the gun, hold starboard, tack early, or wait for the shift, which is how you carry out the first move; change the plan if, one clear thing; how much risk, careful, normal or bold. Say all of them out loud before the five-minute signal.

The card can be paper in a pocket, words written on the deck, or a picture of this page with the answers marked. What matters is that it is filled in before the start and read again afterwards.

On the waterBefore your next race, fill in all nine lines of the before-the-start half, out loud or on the deck after the look and the head-to-wind test, and say “not sure” where you have no evidence. You’ve got it when you can say your nine answers before every race and, for each of the first five, the thing you saw that gave you the answer.

21.2One first move

The fifth line is the one that matters most, and it is one decision: which side first. Go left, go right, or stay in the middle. One first move is enough, and two is none.

The first move comes from the lines above it. More wind on the left and a one-way shift to the left: go left. A swinging day with nothing to choose: the middle, and tack on the headers (chapter 4). The favoured end and the side should agree. When they do not, a strong side wins and you start at the end nearest it. A weak side and a strong end: take the end. Both weak: the middle, in the front row (chapter 9).

Then stick to it. The first move names the side; the after-the-gun line names the tack that gets you there. The first minute is for speed and a lane (chapter 13), and at one minute the card takes over again: hold, tack early, or wait for the shift, whichever carries out the first move. Your change-if line moves your place on the line, not your side. Only a change in the wind you can see changes the side: a shift that has settled, or pressure that has moved (chapter 14).

On a swinging day a header is a reason to tack, not to give up the side: when a real header has stayed and the new tack takes you towards your side, tack at once; when the new tack would take you away from your side, give the header a few seconds more, so that the zigzag leans your way without chasing wobbles (chapter 4). On a one-way day the header on the way out is expected: it is the shift you are sailing towards, so keep going, step by step and never to the layline (chapter 4). The sign that the day has changed is the other one: the tack that should be lifted is being headed, which means the wind has turned back. Then it is a new look (chapter 14).

On the waterBefore your next race, say your first move out loud in three words, “go left”, “go right” or “middle”, and the one change in the wind that would change it. You’ve got it when you can say your first move and the change in the wind that would change it before every race, and after the race you can say whether you did it, and if not, what in the wind changed it.

21.3After the race: one honest reason

The second half of the card is filled in straight after the race, while it is fresh, before the stories start. Three lines. My start: good, ok, or bad. Main reason: wrong side, missed shift, dirty air, slow, early layline, or good race. Next time: one thing, such as look up more, tack on the header, find a gap, go the other way, or go at ten seconds.

One honest reason beats a long story. The memory offers many parts and most of them are excuses; one reason makes you choose what actually decided the beat, and the choice is what you can practise. Honest matters as much as one: a bad start from the wrong end and a bad start from being slow at the gun are different lessons.

Then the question from chapter 16, for your biggest choice or two of the beat: good choice or bad choice, and did it work or not. Two answers, kept apart. “Good choice, did not work” is a fine answer when you can say what you saw that made the choice good; “bad choice, worked” is the one to watch.

Read the before-the-start half again while you do it. Did you start where the card said? Did you make the first move? If not, which reason changed it, and was it one of the reasons you had written down? That is the card's part of the debrief, two minutes on the way in; chapter 23 adds the five questions.

On the waterAfter your next race, before you talk to anyone, fill in the three after-the-race lines and the good-choice question, out loud or on the card. You’ve got it when you can give the three answers and the two-part choice answer after every race within five minutes of finishing, and the reason is one you could show your coach the evidence for.

21.4Draw the first beat

On the back of the card, draw the first beat: the line, the mark, your track, and the winner's track. The drawing is where the learning is.

Keep it simple. A line at the bottom, a dot for the mark at the top, two laylines if you like. Then your track, with a small mark at every tack, and the winner's track beside it, as near as you can remember. If you cannot remember the winner's, draw the track of a boat you could see that beat you.

Now look at the two shapes. Where did they separate? Did you go further to a side than the winner, and did the shift reward it or punish it? Did you tack more often, or less? Where did you reach the layline, and where did the winner? The difference between the two lines is usually one thing, and it is usually the main reason you wrote on the other side of the card.

Keep the drawings. After ten races you have ten shapes, and the shapes show your habit: the corner you always go to, the layline you always reach early, the first tack you always make too late. One race cannot show you that; ten drawings can. Chapter 23 turns them into a score.

On the waterAfter your next race, draw the first beat on the back of the card: the line, the mark, your track with every tack marked, and the winner's track, and circle where they separated. You’ve got it when you have a drawing for every race of the last month, and you can point at the place in each one where the beat was decided.

21.5Pocket cards

The race card is for before and after. For during, the coaching manuals give pocket cards: a few words for each moment of the race, said out loud, in order. A card you can carry is a plan that survives seventy boats.

Before the warning signal: wind, course, start, risk. What are the headings doing: swinging, one-way, or not sure, and is it a day when more wind decides (chapter 4, section 4.6)? Which is the long tack, where is the current, which side is strong? Which end, which transit, where is my first place on the line and my second? How sure am I, and which boats matter?

The final minute: flag, time, line, gap, speed, lane (chapters 11 and 12). The first sixty seconds: legal, fast, lane, then the card (chapters 11 and 13). Every meeting: rights, speed, the lane I end up in (chapter 16). The upper third: reduce leverage, stop and ask, starboard plan, late layline (chapters 14 and 17).

And the card for when you are not sure, which is the one to read most: keep the boat fast, in clear air, near the middle, on the long tack, with the next decision still open. That one sentence is most of this book, and it fits on the back of your hand.

Learn the pocket cards one at a time, the when-not-sure card first, until each one comes out on its own at its moment.

On the waterWrite the when-not-sure card on your hand or your deck before your next race, and read it out loud at the first moment you do not know what to do. You’ve got it when you can say the when-not-sure line without looking, and your coach has heard you say it on the water.
