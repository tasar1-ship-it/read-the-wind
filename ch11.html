Chapter 9 chose the end and the side, and chapter 10 the place in the fleet and the lane. This chapter is the last five minutes themselves, from the warning signal to the gun.

11.1A routine for five minutes

The last five minutes are the same every race, so give them the same routine every race. The sequence is five minutes long: a flag and a sound at five minutes, the , a second flag at four, that flag down at one, and the gun at zero S3. Check the sailing instructions: some events use another sequence. Take the time from the flags, never from a guess.

Five to four: start your watch at the warning signal, read the four-minute flag when it goes up (chapter 12 says what each one means), and say your start card out loud once more. Four to three: sail your approach once, from your chosen place to the line, and count how long you need to reach full speed today.

Three to two: go to the part of the line you chose and pick your neighbours (chapter 10). Two to one: stay close enough to reach the line, but keep room to slow down and turn. One to zero: your , your , the boat above you, and at ten seconds, go.

A routine does the thinking before the thinking gets hard. In the last minute there is no time for any decision you could have made at four. Say each step out loud as you do it, like a pilot: “four minutes, flag up, run-up.” It sounds silly on the water, and it works.

On the waterIn your next start, say each step of the routine out loud at its minute: “five: watch, card”, “four: run-up”, “three: neighbours”, “two: room”, “one: transit, gap”. You’ve got it when you can say the five steps in order without looking at anything, your coach has heard you say them in training starts, and after every race you can say what you did at each minute.

11.2Check again: wind, pressure and end

The head-to-wind test you did at ten minutes is old news at four. The wind shifts, and a shift of five degrees moves the by several boat lengths, sometimes to the other end. So check again just after the warning signal: in the middle, “pin”, “boat” or “square”, and how much.

Check the too. Look up the course for the dark water (chapter 1): is the side you chose still the side with more wind, or has the dark patch moved? And the pattern: on a swinging day, which way is the wind now, left or right, and when did it last change? A wind that has been right for a while may go left before the gun.

If something has changed, change the card, out loud, while there is still time to move: “now boat end, go right.” A plan made at ten minutes and kept out of pride is the most common way to start at the wrong end.

If nothing has changed, say that too: “still pin, still left.” The check that finds nothing is not wasted. It is the one that lets you stop thinking about the end and start thinking about your gap.

On the waterJust after the warning signal, do the head-to-wind test again and look up the course for the dark water. Say out loud whether the end or the side has changed. You’ve got it when after the race you can say what the check after the warning signal found, and when it found something, your coach saw you move.

11.3Gaps open late

At two minutes the line looks full. Every space seems taken, and a sailor who gives up then goes to the second row. But the row is never still: boats luff to slow down and drift sideways, boats lose their nerve and sag back, boats give up on a pile and leave it, and every one of those moves opens a hole somewhere.

Most of the holes open between two minutes and one, and the biggest at the edges of the piles, where the boats that counted eight or more (chapter 10) are leaving. So do not take the second row at two minutes. Stay in the with your bow free, sail slowly along it if you must, and watch for the hole.

A hole is yours when you can get your bow into it before anyone else, with room to and stop, and a gap below you to bear away into later. A hole with another boat already pointing at it from behind is not yours.

The late hole is also a trap: a boat still hunting for one at thirty seconds has no runway left. So hunt until one minute. After that, make your gap where you are (section 11.5).

On the waterIn your next start, between two minutes and one minute, say out loud each time a hole opens near you: “hole, left of the grey boat.” Take one if it is yours. You’ve got it when you can name two holes that opened in the last two minutes of a start, and whether each one was yours.

11.4Your final approach

There are two ways into your place. On starboard, from the right of your place: you come along behind the row heading left, sail half out, head up into your hole and sit there slow with the sail flapping. On starboard the port-tack boats keep clear of you S1, and so does the boat above you once you are alongside her S2; the boat below you is the one you keep clear of.

On port, from behind the line: you sail along behind the row heading right, looking at the holes, and tack into one. In a big fleet it is the risky one: every boat in the row is on starboard and you must keep clear of all of them S1, the tack takes time and room, and the hole you saw can close while you turn.

So in a big fleet the starboard approach is the normal one, and the port approach is for finding a hole late, when your place has gone and you need a new one fast. Either way, arrive early: at two minutes you want to be in your place, not on your way to it.

Arrive slowly as well. A boat that comes in fast has to stop, and a boat that stops by heading up hard turns head to wind, loses steering, and the bow swings on into the boat above. Come in at half speed, with the sail half out, and you can stop where you choose.

On the waterBefore your next start, say which approach you will use, starboard from the right or port from behind, and at two minutes be in your place at half speed. You’ve got it when at two minutes you are in your place, stopped where you chose, in most of your starts.

11.5Make a gap and keep it

Now the : open water below you, a boat length or two, your for the last ten seconds. You make it by slowing down more than the boat below you: head up until the sail flaps, and let her sail on. Every metre she gains ahead and to leeward is a metre of runway for you.

Keeping it is the harder part, because every boat behind you wants it. A boat that gets in below you before the gun has your , and you must keep clear of her S2. So close the door before she gets there: when a boat comes at your gap from behind, a little into it, so there is no room for her bow, then head back up as she goes behind you. Do it early and slowly, while her bow is still well behind your stern, so that she has room to keep clear S7.

Watch the boat above you as well. If she is level with you she cannot take your gap, but she can roll over you at the gun if you are slow. Stay level with her bow, and if she drops back, treat her as a boat coming from behind.

Thirty seconds: sail flapping, gap below, boat above level, bow free. That is the picture to hold until the ten-second go.

On the waterIn your next start, from one minute, hold a gap below you of at least a boat length. Each time a boat comes for it, bear away a little to close the door, then head back up. You’ve got it when at ten seconds you still have a gap of a length or more below you, in most of your starts.

11.6Time, distance, speed, space: go at 10 seconds

A good start needs four things at once: the time to the gun, the distance to the line, your speed, and the space to use it. Time alone gives you a boat that is over or late. Speed without space gives you a collision. Space without speed gives you a boat that is rolled at the gun. The four come together in the last ten seconds.

At ten seconds, from almost stopped, bear away into your gap and sheet in. An Optimist needs about ten seconds to reach full speed (chapter 9), and in those ten seconds it covers about three boat lengths. So at ten seconds you want about three lengths of sailing between you and the line, on your transit, with the gap below you.

Three is today's number only if the wind and the waves agree. In light wind it takes longer to get going, so you go earlier, and often from fewer lengths; in chop, longer still. That is why section 11.1 has you sail the approach at four minutes and count: today's number goes on the card.

Eyes in the last ten seconds: the far end on your transit, the gap, the bow of the boat above you, the far end again. Nothing else. The count is in your head; the rest is in the water in front of you.

On the waterAt four minutes before your next start, from stopped, sheet in, count the seconds to full speed and note how far you went against a mark on the water. Say today's number out loud and start your go that many lengths from the line. You’ve got it when you cross the line within a few seconds of the gun at full speed, in most of your starts, and you can say today's number before each one.

11.7Know your escape

Not every start goes to plan. The hole closes, a boat takes your gap, the boat above rolls you, or the whole middle sags and the front row is three lengths ahead of you at thirty seconds. The question is not whether to escape but when: at thirty seconds you still have choices, at five seconds you have none.

Know your escape before you need it. The usual one is out the back: bear away hard, pass behind the boat below you, and sail along behind the row to the next hole or to the thin place. You lose a few lengths, but you keep your speed, and at the next hole or the thin place you can start in clear air.

The second is to tack out onto port and sail behind the row the other way. It needs room above you and no starboard boat close behind, because you keep clear of her S1, so in a big fleet it is rarely there. The third is to take the second row where you are, and that is only right when the gun is seconds away and the row ahead of you is thin.

The sign that you need one is simple: at thirty seconds you cannot see a way to cross the line on time, at full speed, with clear air. Ask the question of the next section then: two noes at thirty seconds mean escape now.

On the waterAt one minute before your next start, say your escape out loud: “out the back, behind the red boat.” At thirty seconds ask “on time, at speed, clear air?”, and if two answers are no, take it. You’ve got it when you can name your escape at one minute in every start, and when you needed one, you took it before twenty seconds.

11.8The ten-second question

One question, asked at thirty seconds for your escape (section 11.7) and again at ten seconds: can I cross on time, at speed, with clear air? It has three parts. On time: my transit says about three lengths back, and the boats beside me are not ahead of me. At speed: I have a gap to bear away into, and the sheet is ready in my hand. Clear air: nobody is ahead and to windward of me, and nobody is about to be.

Three yeses: go. Bear away, sheet in, hike, with your eyes on the far end, the gap and the boat above you. One no: you still go, because at ten seconds the alternatives are worse, but you go with a plan for the first minute. A no on clear air means speed first for thirty seconds, then the way out of chapter 13: a hole behind for a tack, or water below to foot into. A no on speed means look for a lane behind the front row. A no on time because you are early means ease the sheet and let the line come to you: late is better than . A no because you are far back means go now, and look for clear air behind the front row.

Two or three noes at ten seconds usually mean the second row, because by then the escape of section 11.7 has gone. It is also a lesson for the card: which part failed, and when? Usually a minute before you noticed.

Say the question out loud at ten seconds, every start. Soon you will not need to say it: you will feel the three answers as you bear away.

On the waterAt ten seconds before your next start, say the question out loud, “on time, at speed, clear air?”, and answer each part yes or no. You’ve got it when you can say your three answers after every start, and when one was no, you can say what you did about it in the first minute.

11.9At the gun

The gun fires. Three checks, in order: two in the first fifteen seconds, the third at thirty. Legal: was I over? If you are not sure, look at the committee boat and listen. If the committee signals that someone was over, chapter 12 tells you what to do, and the sooner you look, the less it costs.

Fast: flat boat, sheet in, hike, eyes on the sail, the water ahead and the boats close to you. For the first thirty seconds speed is the only thing you control and the only thing that keeps a lane. Every boat that is slow now is in ten seconds later.

Lane: at thirty seconds, look up at the fleet and ask whether the will last. A boat ahead and to windward, a boat close below pointing higher, a bow creeping up behind your windward side: each one is a lane dying. Chapter 13 is about what to do then. For now: decide early, while you still have speed, not after it has gone.

Then the plan. The after-the-gun line on your card: hold, tack early, or wait for the shift. If the start gave you your lane, do it. If it did not, speed first, lane second, plan third, and the plan comes back as soon as you have clear air.

On the waterAt your next start, say “legal” and “fast” out loud in the first fifteen seconds and “lane” at thirty. Then say your after-the-gun move and do it. You’ve got it when your coach hears “legal, fast, lane” from the coach boat in training starts, after every race you can say what each check found, and a minute after the gun you are doing the after-the-gun move on your card, or you can say why not.
